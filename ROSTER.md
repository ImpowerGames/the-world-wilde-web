# Roster — locked

**Boundary rule** (rewritten 2026-08-05, per Lovelle). An edge exists only when **both** endpoints are on this roster — that part stands. What changed is who gets to be on it: **every person the record documents as a partner, spouse or object of attraction is a node.** The old rule sent them to a person's `context_engagements` instead, and that slot turned out to be a holding pen rather than a scope lock. It hid fourteen people inside other people's files — among them a bookmaker who shared three years and three addresses with Fred Atkins, a boy bought from his father for 200 lire, and four legal wives — so a reader looking at the drawing could not see them at all. **The scope lock is now the connection, not the person**: someone joins because a documented connection to someone already here puts them in the record, which still keeps Natalie Barney's whole Paris circle and Lillie Langtry's royal one out. `context_engagements` survives for engagements that are genuinely not person-to-person — collective mentions, and pointers with nobody identifiable at the other end.

**Change control.** Adding a person = a deliberate roster edit here, not a research-session side effect. Candidates go to the parking lot below.

**The exile-years liaison tier IS on the roster** (decided 2026-08-03, per Lovelle; this reverses an earlier call to keep them as a collective footnote). Every distinct documented person Wilde had a romantic or sexual connection with gets a node, whether or not the record preserves a name: André, Pietro, "le petit Georges", Raphael, Fortuné, the fisherman at Napoule, the Naples "Sea-God", the Cairo-café Egyptian.

*Why the reversal.* The earlier rule kept them off on grounds of "no stable identity" — but the roster already carries Charles Parker, Alfred Wood and Fred Atkins as full nodes, working-class men preserved only because a criminal court wrote them down. Excluding the men preserved instead in Wilde's letters would make the map reproduce an archival accident: connections survive as nodes when the state recorded them and vanish when it didn't. The historical point of the chart is better served by including them.

*Rules for this tier.* A node needs a distinct documented **person** — a plural or collective mention ("the fishing population", "the fisher-lads") stays in the collective note on `wilde`, since no individual is identifiable. Where the source gives no name, the node is labelled by what the record actually provides, marked plainly as a descriptor rather than a name (e.g. "A fisherman, Napoule"), and the person record says so. Group: `later` (the exile years). Ages are stated exactly as the source states them and never inferred.

**Eolo is now a node** (2026-08-05, reversing the 2026-08-03 call to keep him a `mellor` context engagement). The reasoning for keeping him off still holds as a description — the letters record Mellor's conduct toward a servant in terms of purchase and ownership, it is not a relationship of Wilde's, and it needs careful framing — but that is an argument about **how to frame the edge, not about whether the boy exists on the map**. Leaving him inside Mellor's file made the purchaser visible and the purchased invisible, which is the archival accident this roster reversed for the exile tier two days earlier. The careful framing now lives where it belongs, in the edge's own `certainty_reasoning` and his `bio_note`, both of which state that this is servitude and not a partnership and that no reciprocity is claimed. Groups drive node color only: `core · family · society · aesthete · trials · chaeronea · later · beyond` (`beyond` = figures outside the London circle romantically linked into it).

## Roster candidates surfaced 2026-08-04

- **Harry Stafford (1858–1918)** — the New Jersey farm boy Whitman attached himself to from 1876, after the Camden move; Whitman gave him a ring, took it back and gave it again. **The lead is sourced but thin in what we hold**: Murray's Whitman Archive entry on Doyle says only that "In New Jersey, Harry Stafford provided Whitman with a measure of the companionship that Doyle was not there to give", and the WHITMAN dossier already carries Ellmann's line that Whitman bragged to Stafford, "perhaps to make Stafford a little jealous", that "Wilde had the good sense to take a great fancy to me." Neither will carry an edge on its own. To add him properly we need Whitman's letters to Stafford — the *Correspondence* ed. Miller, or the Whitman Archive transcriptions, which are open but were not consulted. **A real second Whitman edge, one source away.** Parked pending that, and pending Lovelle.
- **An unnamed boy in grey velvet, Paris (late March 1900)** — seen with Wilde outside a cafe behind the Madeleine by Aleck Ross; *Complete Letters* printed 1177. He was the only person in the retired collective 'one-scene companions' entry on Wilde who does not already have a node. He stands on the same footing as the Naples 'Sea-God' and the fisherman at Napoule, both of whom are nodes, so admitting him would be consistent — but adding a person is Lovelle's call, so he is parked here rather than created.

## Candidates surfaced by the *Complete Letters* sweep, 2026-08-04

A mechanical pass over the complete OCR mirror (1,315 pages, no gaps): every letter heading
extracted — **514 distinct recipients, 1,441 letters** — cross-referenced against the roster, then
scored against a desire lexicon and, separately, an occupation lexicon aimed at the working-class
men who appear only in passing. **Everything below was then read at the rendered page.** Adding a
person is Lovelle's call, so these are parked, not created.

### 1. Richard Le Gallienne (1866–1947) — the strongest new candidate
Poet and critic; **14 letters** in the volume. Wilde to Le Gallienne, postmark 1 December 1890,
16 Tite Street, printed **p. 457** (read at page):

> I want so much to see you: when can that be?  Friendship and love like ours need not meetings,
> but they are delightful.  I hope your laurels are not too thick across your brow for me to kiss
> your eyelids.  Always yours OSCAR

Shape fits `attraction-expressed` exactly: the record is one-sided — Le Gallienne's replies are not in
this volume — and the language is legible as desire without needing the coding hypothesis.
**Recommended.**

### 2. The Reading ex-prisoners — a cluster the map has no one from
Three named working-class men Wilde helped after release. All read at page.

- **Harry Elvin** — printed **p. 976**, to Reggie Turner, postmark 30 October 1897, from Naples.
  Wilde borrows £2.10 to send him: *"I have one friend left in Reading, the one I liked best; a
  very handsome young soldier of twenty years of age – Harry Elvin.  I promised him I would leave a
  letter at the Post Office for him and he would be greatly disappointed not to hear from me, as we
  always walked together, and had long chats."* Named, aged, described, and the money is borrowed
  rather than spare. The warmest of the three.
- **Arthur Cruttenden** — printed ~p. 902, to Turner, June 1897: Wilde sends him to Turner, *"a very
  simple nice fellow"*.
- **Lawley** — printed ~p. 899: Wilde recommends him for work, *"a quiet well-conducted chap"*, a
  Third Hussars man who *"would be a good groom"*.

**The honest problem, for Lovelle to settle.** This map documents *romantic and sexual*
relationships. What these three letters document is affection plus material care — Wilde, broke in
Naples, borrowing money for a man still inside. Elvin's *"the one I liked best"* and *"very
handsome"* are fond and admiring; they do not clear the README's bar that desire be legible as
desire. Read strictly they are the `mellor--wilde` shape, which is recorded as a **null**
(patronage, not romance). But the boundary rule also means that leaving them off keeps the map's
poorest men invisible while its richest are fully drawn — which is the pattern the Portraits
section already admits to. **Three options: promote Elvin alone; promote all three; or record all
three as a named collective `context_engagement` on Wilde, as the exile liaison tier was before it
was un-parked.** Not decided here.
