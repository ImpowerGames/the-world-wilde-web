# Contributing

This is a map of documented queer relationships during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. If you know a corner of it we have not covered, please add it. You can contribute new information, quotes, sources, images, or anything else that might be useful for the map.

We especially welcome corrections. If a citation is wrong, a transcription drifts, or a classification overstates its evidence, we want to fix it.

## Two ways to contribute

- **[Open a ticket]({{repo}}/issues)**. Tell us what you know and we'll update the site accordingly. This is the right path if you have found an error, know of a source we have not read, or know of a person or a connection that belongs on the map.
- **[Submit a pull request]({{repo}}/blob/HEAD/CONTRIBUTING-CODE.md)**. If you are comfortable editing JSON data directly or wish to contribute something to the code of the site itself, you are welcome to submit a pull request.

## Evidentiary standards

Make sure your claims are appropriately evidenced. We will not accept:

- A claim with no citation behind it.
- A quotation that was only remembered or read in a search index, rather than verified directly on the page.
- A claim that reads absence of evidence as evidence itself, in either direction.
- A claim that infers an age or date that the source does not specifically state.

## What to put in a ticket

The issue templates will ask for these details, but in short — a ticket we can act on says:

- **What the source actually says.** Quote it. A few sentences of surrounding context is better than one clipped phrase, because the context is usually what shows who is speaking and about whom.
- **Where to find it.** Author, title, year, and a page — or a manuscript reference, an archive and a shelfmark, a URL. Enough that someone else can look it up and verify.
- **How you saw it.** Read on the page, in a scan, in a search index, or quoted in another book. All four are useful, but they are not equally strong. We would rather know which it was than guess.
- **An image of the document when possible.** See [Transcription guidelines](#transcription-guidelines) — if you read the words off a manuscript, attach the page or link to it, so someone else can check the reading.

If you do not have all of that, open a ticket anyway. A pointer to where the evidence may be is better than nothing.

You do not need to argue for a particular classification. Just say what the source says and where you found it, and we will do our best to classify the connection accordingly.

## Quotes & Sources

**For letters, quote the whole letter**. If the letter is long, you may also include a shortened version that elides anything that neither characterizes the relationship nor provides useful context (marking each cut with an ellipsis). But always include the full letter in the ticket alongside your shortened version.

**Friendship counts.** A quotation does not have to reach romance to be worth recording, and a person does not have to carry a connection line to belong on the map. Warmth between individuals in this period is part of what the record shows and part of what the map is for; George Alexander and Graham Hill are here as nodes with no line, holding letters that are simply fond. Record the friendship, let the certainty class stay empty, and let a reader see the affection and make judgments for themselves.

**Read it at the page yourself.** Either in a physical copy or a digital reproduction. If you are using a digital scan or PDF, always cite the page number printed on the original document, not the page number in your PDF viewer, as these frequently differ. Do not rely on OCR. An OCR mirror's pagination can often drift by a few pages, causing citations to be inaccurate. Use OCR only as a finding aid, and cite directly from the page.

## Transcription guidelines

_(Adapted from the [Massachusetts Historical Society transcription guide](https://www.masshist.org/mymhs/transcription-guide).)_

**Include an image of the document in your ticket wherever you can, especially if the document is handwritten.** A transcription of a handwritten document is a reading, and readings differ — the difference between `shall not` and `should not`, or between `men` and `man` under a smudge, can change what a letter means. If we can see the page we can check you, and later contributors can check us. Without an image your transcription can still be recorded, but it cannot be marked as verified against the document.

If you cannot share the image give us a link to where it can be seen instead: the archive's viewer, a catalogue record, a IIIF manifest, an auction listing.

Where the writing is genuinely hard, say so and say where. e.g. "The last two lines cross a fold and I am not confident of the final word" tells us far more than a clean transcription that hides the doubt.

**Transcribe original spelling, punctuation, capitalization, special characters, word order, underlines, accents, and marks exactly as they appear.** Do not correct spelling, punctuation or grammar. Do not expand abbreviations. Douglas's `En revange` keeps his mistake; Wilde's `thro'`, `Recd.` and `12. o'c.` stay contracted where the printed editions expand them.

Exceptions:

- When a word is split between lines (this type of word may or may not have a hyphen), please transcribe the full word without line breaks or hyphens: `res-olutions` becomes `resolutions`.
- Hyphens that are used to connect two textual components into a single word should be transcribed without any extra space (e.g., `anti-slavery`, `post-dated`, `type-writer`).
- Dashes are sometimes used as periods and sometimes used as dashes. Transcribe every clause-separating dash as an em dash with one space either side — `and I have had two of them — and since I arrived`. Do not convert them to full stops like the printed editions do.
- Writers of this era often use `+` as shorthand for `and`. Always transcribe `+` as `&` for shorthand and's. An ampersand already written as `&` stays `&` — do not expand `+` or `&` to "and", even though the printed editions do.
- Start a new line for a paragraphs, but do not indicate line breaks, indents, or other spacing. Because of this, a mark that exists only because of where the line ended is dropped: Wilde wrote the Ballad's title across three lines and repeated the quotation mark at each break —

```
             'The
'Ballad of Reading'
Gaol'
```

— which is a line-wrap convention, not four quotations. It normalizes to one opening and one closing mark: `'The Ballad of Reading Gaol'`.

### Interpreting Handwriting

Editors who publish printed editions of handwritten letters often prioritize readability over strict visual accuracy. While they are typically meticulous in deciphering letterforms, they tend to be less precise with formatting marks. Because experienced editors like Holland have read hundreds of these letters and intimately know the author's hand, you can generally trust their transcription of the words themselves. However, editors frequently standardize formatting — italicizing foreign words or book titles — without distinguishing those additions from words the author explicitly underlined for emphasis. Therefore, when analyzing marks and formatting, always consult the original manuscript as your source of truth:

| For                                                             | Generally trust                            |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **Letterforms:** spelling, capitals, plurals, and word identity | The **printed edition** (where one exists) |
| **Marks:** underlines, strikethroughs, insertions, and dashes   | The **original document**                  |

_(When an ordinary English word is printed in italics, it is strong evidence that it was underlined in the original document. However, always consult the manuscript to verify.)_

#### Resolving Discrepancies

When your reading of a word differs from the printed edition, evaluate which version makes more sense in context:

- **If the edition's reading is more sensible:** An editor who has read hundreds of these letters is unlikely to misread a common word. You are probably misreading a tricky letterform and should re-examine the manuscript to verify.
- **If your reading is more sensible:** You may have caught a genuine editorial error. For example, on page 1197 of the _Complete Letters_, the print reads _"Cheque takes ages to cash,"_ whereas the original sheet clearly reads _"cheques take ages to cash"_ — matching the plural subject to a plural verb for natural English syntax.

#### Decoding Wilde's Penmanship

Wilde writes lowercase `t`'s with a tall stroke with a long crossbar that sits much higher on the stem than other writers. This makes his lowercase `t`'s notoriously difficult to distinguish from his capital `T`s.

- **Before recording a mid-sentence capital:** Check how the printed edition transcribed it.
- **If no printed edition exists:** Find a word on the same sheet that must contain a lowercase `t` (like the `tt` in _letter_, or the `t` in _to_) and a word that must start with a capital (a title, a person's name, a proper noun) and use both as a baseline for comparison.

Use brackets for any text that is your voice rather than the writer's:

| Form             | Means                                                             | Example                             |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| `[…?]`           | your best guess at a word you cannot read with confidence         | `and what a bore, the [man?]`       |
| `[illegible]`    | you cannot read it at all                                         | `your [illegible] friend`           |
| `[inserted: …]`  | the writer added this above or below the line, usually on a caret | `to pay the [inserted: whole] bill` |
| `[annotated: …]` | the writer's own marginal note or annotation                      | `[annotated: h.s.]`                 |

Surround struck-through or scribbled-over text with `~~strikethrough~~`. If you are unsure of what was struck, use `~~[…?]~~` for your best guess or `~~[illegible]~~` if its truly unreadable. Where a word was struck and replaced, keep both: `~~concious~~ [inserted: conscience]`. A cancellation is evidence — the subject matter here was criminally prosecutable, and a struck-out word is sometimes a writer censoring themself.

**Do not include annotations that the writer themself did not make.** Later hands are all over this material: a bold `Forged` written across the head of one letter, `Blackmailer` down the margin of another, pencil crosses bracketing the sentence a dealer wanted to quote. None of these later additions belongs in the quotation; describe it alongside instead, and say whose hand it is if you can tell. Where a later hand has obscured the writer's words, transcribe the words and record the obliteration.

### Emphasis Markup

**Whatever mark is on a page is the mark that goes in the quote.** If it is underlined in the letter, underline it in the quote. If it is struck through, strike it through. If the writer put quotation marks round a title, keep them — even where the printed edition has taken them away. Mark it inside the quotation with `*italics*`, `**bold**`, `~~strikethrough~~`, `_underline_`, `__double underline__`. **The number of underscores is the number of underlines.**

**The marks matter more here than in most archives.** The subject matter was criminally prosecutable, and writers often self-censored on the page: a struck-out word can be a trace of that happening. Wilde also underlined words to flag them as code or euphemism to a particular reader.

**A printed edition cannot settle emphasis. Check the original handwritten document when collating.** The Complete Letters of Oscar Wilde (2000) says of their transcriptions: _"The printing of titles has been standardised: those of poems, stories and articles are printed in roman type between quotation marks; those of books, plays, periodicals and ships in italics. All foreign words are printed in italics unless the whole letter is in French. All underlined words are italicised, but no indication is given of the occasional words which have more than one underlining."_ One italic therefore stands for three unrelated things — an underline, a title, a foreign word — and the difference between one underline and three is thrown away. So an italic on the printed page tells you almost nothing; only the manuscript does.

**Italics are the one place we depart from the manuscript on purpose.** Handwritten text cannot italicize. The only emphasis marks available to someone writing by hand are underlines. Italics are therefore applied here as an editorial consistency — books, plays, periodicals, ships, foreign words — on top of whatever the hand did. Where the writer underlined the title as well, both marks are kept:

| In the manuscript                                       | Write                            | Reads as                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| `do` with one underline under it                        | `_do_`                           | stress, in the writer's own hand           |
| `me` with two underlines under it                       | `__me__`                         | stress, doubled                            |
| _Salomé_ with an underline under it                     | `_*Salomé*_`                     | a title, and he underlined it              |
| `'Ballad of Reading Gaol'`, single quotes, no underline | `'*The Ballad of Reading Gaol*'` | his quotation marks kept, our italic added |
| a title nobody has checked yet                          | `*Lady Windermere's Fan*`        | title italicized by convention;            |

**Typed and printed sources are matched exactly.** A typescript, a printed book, a typed transcript can all italicize, embolden, underline and strike through, so our editorial convention is unnecessary — reproduce what is set, mark for mark. Note that all marks are the _typist's_, not the writer's: several documents here are typed copies of letters whose autographs are elsewhere, and no claim about the writer's own emphasis can rest on them.

**Not every horizontal stroke is an underline.** A long diagonal between paragraphs is a paragraph mark. A rule under an interlineated phrase is an insertion caret. A stroke across a printed letterhead cancels the letterhead. Wilde's `t` is drawn with a long crossbar that lands over or under the neighbouring word, and his terminal `e` in `the` trails a long horizontal tail. A dash on the following line can sit exactly where a second underline would. Ruled or squared paper, and show-through from the other side, may give the impression of an underline where there is none.

A real underline is the width of the word, at the weight of the writing, in the ink of the writing. Shape is not part of the test: they run from short and flat to long and sweeping. If you are not sure, say so rather than deciding — mark it in your ticket as uncertain and let someone look at the page.
