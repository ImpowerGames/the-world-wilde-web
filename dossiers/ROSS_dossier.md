## wilde — lover per later Ross statements (dating contested); lifelong closest friend; literary executor

### Cluster A — first surviving letter + editors' main-particulars footnote (printed p. 360)

**Passage 1 — first surviving letter to Ross**

> My dear Bobbie, I congratulate you.   University life will suit you admirably, though I shall miss you in town.   Enclosed is the praise of the Philistines.   Are you in College or in lodgings?   I hope in College; it is much nicer.   Do you know Oscar Browning?   You will find him everything that is kind and pleasant.
>
> I have been speaking at Stratford about Shakespeare, but in spite of that enjoyed my visit immensely.   My reception was semi-royal, and the volunteers played God Save the Queen in my honour.   Ever yours   OSCAR WILDE

— Wilde to Robert Ross, [Circa 13 October 1888], 16 Tite Street. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 360.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 382 (printed p. 360), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** earliest surviving letter of the correspondence; established intimacy by Oct 1888 ("My dear Bobbie … I shall miss you in town"); NOTE the affectionate but non-sexual surface register.

**Passage 2 — editors' main-particulars footnote on Ross (360 n.1) — copyrighted editorial matter, quoted ≤2 sentences**

> Robert Baldwin Ross (1869–1918) was a Canadian. … He first met Wilde in 1886 and remained his most faithful friend until Wilde's death when he became the literary executor to the estate.

— Holland & Hart-Davis, editorial note, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 360 n.1.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 382 (printed p. 360), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision
**Supports:** Ross born 1869, died 1918; met Wilde 1886; "most faithful friend"; literary executor. Also on the page (not quoted, paraphrase for dossier): grandfather Robert Baldwin first Prime Minister of Upper Canada; father John Ross, Attorney General; brought to England aged two; went up to King's College, Cambridge, 13 October 1888 to read History; left Cambridge abruptly 1889; became literary journalist and art critic; footnote cites Margery Ross (ed.), _Robert Ross: Friend of Friends_ (1952) and Maureen Borland, _Wilde's Devoted Friend_ (1990). **The footnote asserts friendship + executorship only — no sexual claim.**

**Passage 3 — Wilde on Ross to a third party (same page)**

> I knew you would like Bobbie Ross.   He is charming, and as clever as can be, with excellent taste and sound knowledge.   I am so glad he is with you.

— Wilde to Oscar Browning, [October 1888], 16 Tite Street. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 360.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 382 (printed p. 360), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Wilde's public register of affection/esteem for Ross in 1888 — corroborates closeness, not sexual dimension.

### Cluster B — July 1889 letters (printed pp. 407–409; folios verified, offset +22 holds: 407=429, 408=430, 409=431)

**Passage 4 — "the story is half yours" (The Portrait of Mr W. H.)**

> Dear Bobbie, Your telegram (of course it was *yours*) has just arrived.   So many thanks for it: it was really sweet of you to send it, for indeed the story is half yours, and but for you would not have been written.   Are you well again?   Terror for Cyril kept me away, but now I may come, may I not?
>
> Write to me a letter.   Now that Willie Hughes has been revealed to the world, we must have another secret.   Ever yours, dear Bobbie   O. W.

— Wilde to Robert Ross, [July 1889], 16 Tite Street. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), pp. 407–8.
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 429–30 (printed pp. 407–8; letter opens at foot of 407, continues on 408), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Ross as co-begetter of 'The Portrait of Mr W. H.' (editors' note 408 n.1: the story appeared in *Blackwood's* July 1889 and "sought to identify the dedicatee of Shakespeare's sonnets as a boy actor called Willie Hughes" — ≤2-sentence editorial quote); "we must have another secret" — shared-secret intimacy register, 1889.

**Passage 5 — "I hate your going away"**

>
> I am going out of town until Monday, but will see you next week, I hope.   I hate your going away.   Ever yours   OSCAR

— Wilde to Robert Ross, [? July 1889], Lyric Club, Piccadilly East. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 408.

— Wilde to Robert Ross, [Late July 1889], Kreuznach. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 409.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 431 (printed p. 409), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision

### Cluster D — Epilogue: Ross's account of Wilde's death, in his letter to MORE ADEY, 14 December 1900 (printed pp. 1219–1222; folios verified, PDF 1264–1267, +45)

The epilogue's main death-account document is headed "Robert Ross to More Adey *(continued from p.1214)*", dated 14 December 1900. (That the most personal document of Ross's life is addressed to Adey is itself evidence for the Ross–Adey bond — cross-filed in ADEY-GILBERT-TURNER_dossier.md.)

**Passage 11 — arrival in Paris; the priest (printed p. 1219)**

> During my absence Reggie went every day to see Oscar, and wrote me short bulletins every other day.   Oscar went out several times with him driving, and seemed much better. … on the Wednesday evening, at five-thirty, I got a telegram from Reggie saying, 'Almost hopeless.'   I just caught the express and arrived in Paris at 10.20 in the morning. … He was conscious that people were in the room, and raised his hand when I asked him whether he understood.   He pressed our hands.   I then went in search of a priest, and after great difficulty found Father Cuthbert Dunne, of the Passionists, who came with me at once and administered Baptism and Extreme Unction — Oscar could not take the Eucharist.

— Robert Ross to More Adey, 14 December 1900. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), pp. 1219–20.
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 1264–65 (printed pp. 1219–20), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision
**Supports:** Ross summoned by telegram; his standing promise to bring Wilde a priest ("You know I had always promised to bring a priest to Oscar when he was dying", p. 1220); Wilde's reception into the Catholic Church arranged by Ross.

**Passage 12 — the death, 30 November 1900 (printed p. 1220)**

> He and I slept at the Hôtel d'Alsace that night in a room upstairs.   We were called twice by the nurse, who thought Oscar was actually dying. … At 12 o'clock I went out to get some food, Reggie mounting guard.   He went out at 12.30.   From 1 o'clock we did not leave the room; the painful noise from the throat became louder and louder.   Reggie and myself destroyed letters to keep ourselves from breaking down. … I went to the bedside and held his hand, his pulse began to flutter.   He heaved a deep sigh, the only natural one I had heard since I arrived, the limbs seemed to stretch involuntarily, the breathing came fainter; he passed at 10 minutes to 2 p.m. exactly.

— Robert Ross to More Adey, 14 December 1900. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 1220.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1265 (printed p. 1220), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision
**Supports:** Ross (with Turner) present at the death; held Wilde's hand as he died. Editors' note 1220 n.1 dates the death Friday, 30 November 1900, and (≤2-sentence editorial quote): "Biographers, notably Richard Ellmann, have conjectured that he was suffering from syphilis, but there is little, if any, evidence for this. The medical report of 27 November (MS Hyde) signed by Claisse and Tucker simply diagnoses cerebral meningitis."

**Passage 13 — laying out; the funeral; the grave in Ross's name (printed pp. 1221–1222)**

> I am glad to say dear Oscar looked calm and dignified, just as he did when he came out of prison, and there was nothing at all horrible about the body after it had been washed.   Around his neck was the blessed rosary which you gave me, and on the breast a Franciscan medal given me by one of the nuns, a few flowers placed there by myself and an anonymous friend …
>
> … we all walked to the Church of St Germain des Prés behind the hearse — Alfred Douglas, Reggie Turner and myself, Dupoirier, the proprietor of the hotel, Henri the nurse, and Jules, the servant of the hotel, Dr Hennion and Maurice Gilbert, together with two strangers whom I did not know. … The drive took one hour and a half; the grave is at Bagneux, in a temporary concession hired in my name — when I am able I shall purchase ground elsewhere, at Père Lachaise for choice.

— Robert Ross to More Adey, 14 December 1900. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), pp. 1221–2.
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 1266–67 (printed pp. 1221–2), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision
**Supports:** Ross as chief mourner and practical custodian (grave hired in his name; later Père Lachaise plan he executed in 1909 per p. 1230 editorial matter); "the blessed rosary which you gave me" — the "you" is Adey (cross-filed). Funeral procession roll-call also names Douglas, Turner, Gilbert.

### Cluster E — Ross to Adela Schuster, 14 December 1900 (printed pp. 1227–1230)

**Passage 7 — THE KEY TIMELINE PASSAGE: Ross's own account of when intimacy began (printed p. 1229)**

> I was not surprised by the silence of the press.   Journalists could hardly say very much, and it was better to be silent than point a moral.   Later on I think everyone will recognise his achievements; his plays and essays will endure.   Of course you may think with others that his personality and conversation were far more wonderful than anything he wrote, so that his written works give only a pale reflection of his power.   Perhaps that is so, and of course it will be impossible to reproduce what is gone for ever.   I am not alas a Boswell, as some friends have kindly suggested I should become.   But I only met him in '86 and only became intimate with him when he was writing *Lady Windermere*, but there were long intervals when I never saw him, and he never corresponded with me regularly until after the downfall.   I stayed with him in '87 for two months and used then to write down what he said, but to tell you a *great secret* which I ought not to do, I gave him my notes and he used a great deal of them for one of his later plays which was written in a great hurry and against time as he wanted money.   This of course is *private*.

— Robert Ross to Adela Schuster, 14 December 1900. Printed in the Epilogue, Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 1229.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1274 (printed p. 1229), read as page image 2026-08-03; key sentences re-verified on 4x zoomed region render (scratchpad p1274_zoom.png) 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact (italics in original: 'Lady Windermere', 'great secret', 'private'; 'met' is NOT italic — first-pass transcription error caught at zoom and corrected)
**Supports:** Ross's OWN timeline: met Wilde 1886; "became intimate with him when he was writing *Lady Windermere*" (i.e. c. 1891); stayed with him two months in 1887; irregular correspondence until after 1895. **This first-person statement COMPLICATES the standard "lover from c. 1886" dating** — whatever "intimate" means here, Ross himself dates the closeness to c. 1891, not 1886. The famous claim that Ross was Wilde's first male lover (c. 1886) comes from later Ross statements reported in secondary literature (another agent's beat), not from this letter.

**Passage 8 — Ross's account of Wilde's last weeks (printed p. 1227)**

> To continue.   I saw him every day and sometimes twice a day from October 15 till November 13th.   He drove out in the Bois with me on several occasions.   On November 1st the doctor took me aside and told me that although he was getting well, his general condition was serious, and that unless he pulled himself up he could not live for more than five years. … However he made plans for coming to the South of France in order to be near me when he was better and Harris had sent him over the money due for his play.

— Robert Ross to Adela Schuster, 14 December 1900. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 1227.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1272 (printed p. 1227), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision
**Supports:** daily attendance October–November 1900; Wilde's plan "to be near me" — the relationship's final-phase centrality.

**Passage 9 — the farewell visit (printed p. 1227)**

> The night before I went away, when I paid a farewell visit, I found Mr Wilde in a very excited state.   He asked Mr Turner and the nurse to leave the room, and then burst into hysterical tears, saying he would never see me again.

— Robert Ross to Adela Schuster, 14 December 1900. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 1227.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1272 (printed p. 1227), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** emotional intensity of the bond on Wilde's side in the final month (also documents Turner's presence in the sickroom).

**Passage 10 — memoir hesitation and the Judas remark (printed p. 1230)**

> Some time I should be very grateful if you allowed me to have your views as to the advisability of a memoir, and its scope or plan, and if done with discretion whether it would please and interest his friends.   I would not care for it to appeal to morbid curiosity, and I remember Mr Wilde's remark 'that it is always Judas who writes the biography'.   You are in a better position to judge of this question, and to gauge the value of the interest taken in his work and career prior to the downfall.   Sincerely yours   ROBERT ROSS

— Robert Ross to Adela Schuster, 14 December 1900. Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 1230.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1275 (printed p. 1230), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Ross as custodian-of-legacy immediately after the death (precursor of the executorship). Dossier context (not quoted): the editors' epilogue matter on the same page records the Bagneux tombstone, the 1909 removal to Père Lachaise under Epstein's monument, and the 'alien tears' quatrain.

**Also on printed p. 1229 (context, not an edge claim):** "A great many people would dislike admitting their former intimacy with him, and if they have kept his letters would not care to lend them." — Ross on OTHERS' reticence, December 1900; useful colour for the post-trials climate.
