# ROSS — WILDE

## The 1886 seduction, and what Fryer does with the dating tension

**Passage F1 — the seduction, the ages, and the chain of testimony (printed 18)**

> Little did Eliza Ross or Constance Wilde suspect, when they finalized this arrangement, that some time the previous year, the thirty-two-year-old Oscar and the seventeen-year-old Robbie had become lovers.

and, further down the same page:

> Whatever the precise circumstances of the contact being forged, it would appear that young Robbie seduced Oscar some time in 1886, and that this was Oscar's first experience of a homosexual act.   Oscar told his friend, the journalist and wit Reggie Turner, that this was the case.   Robbie himself later confirmed it to one of Wilde's earliest biographers, Arthur Ransome.

— Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 18.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 32, printed folio **18** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact (two separated blocks on one page, quoted separately, not elided together)
**Supports:** Fryer plants himself squarely on the traditional 1886 dating and adds a *chain of participant testimony* for it — Wilde told Turner; Ross told Ransome. Ages stated exactly as Fryer states them. The "arrangement" is Ross's 1887 lodging at 16 Tite Street.
**Register note:** Fryer's account makes Ross the initiator ("young Robbie seduced Oscar"), reversing the usual older-man script; his jacket copy (PDF p. 2, unpaginated) calls Ross "the young man who first seduced Oscar Wilde".

**Passage F2 — Fryer's own qualification, and the accidental reconciliation of the tension (printed 21)**

> It should not be assumed, however, that Oscar and Robbie conducted a passionate, clandestine affair on the premises.   On the contrary, it is unlikely that they did, and not only for reasons of propriety.   Despite the initial seduction, and the huge bond of affection between them, neither was physically each other's 'type'.

— Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 21.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 35, printed folio **21** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Fryer distinguishes a single initiating episode in 1886 from a sustained sexual relationship, and denies the latter for the 1887 Tite Street months. He continues that Ross "was far more interested sexually in youths of his own age than in the rather portly, flabby, albeit fascinating Oscar Wilde."
**Why this matters for the dating tension:** Fryer never cites, quotes or engages Ross's 14 December 1900 letter to Adela Schuster ("I only met him in '86 and only became intimate with him when he was writing _Lady Windermere_"). His notes cite a *different* Schuster letter (RR to Adela Schuster, 23 December 1900). So he does not resolve the tension **explicitly**. But F1+F2 read together supply a structure in which both statements can be true: an initiating episode in 1886, no sustained affair through the Tite Street period, the settled intimacy later. Whether that reconciliation is Fryer's or ours is a judgement for the roster owner — he does not say it.

**Passage F3 — "sometime lover", at nineteen (printed 33)**

> In fact, Robbie was already, at the age of nineteen, much more than a disciple and sometime lover for Oscar.   The two could talk frankly, despite the difference in their ages, and bounce ideas off each other.

— Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 33.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 47, printed folio **33** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Fryer's own summary formula for the relationship at the turn of 1888–89: "disciple and sometime lover". **"Sometime"** is the operative word and is Fryer's, not ours — it is his second signal (with F2) that he does not read the bond as a continuous affair. Age stated exactly as Fryer states it.

**Passage F4a — "the sort of love he felt for Oscar" (printed 42)**

> Robbie seems to have felt no jealousy towards John Gray.   Indeed, he was remarkably generous and understanding when it came to Oscar's other lovers.   Of course, he himself was enjoying various encounters with youths, though none at this stage held any claim to his affections, let alone to the sort of love he felt for Oscar.

— Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 42.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 56, printed folio **42** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** c. 1889–90. Fryer's account of the asymmetry that makes the rest of Ross's life legible: Ross in love with Wilde, unpossessive about Gray and Wilde's other lovers, and sexually occupied elsewhere. Same page: the family council that sent Ross to Edinburgh, away from "myriad temptations, including Oscar Wilde", after his sexuality was "broached in the Ross household".

They made an odd couple when they walked out together. # ROSS — DOUGLAS

**Passage F6 — Ross's own ranking of the three men (printed 198)**

> Relations between Bosie and Robbie were deteriorating fast.   Though Robbie once said that the three most important men in his life had been Oscar Wilde, Bosie Douglas and Freddie Smith, by the end of 1907, relations with Bosie were distinctly frosty.

— Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 198.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 220, printed folio **198** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Reported first-person statement by Ross placing Douglas beside his two documented lovers, Wilde and Freddie Smith, as the three most important men in his life. **Careful:** the claim Fryer reports is *importance*, not romance, and he gives it as indirect speech with no note number at the sentence — no source is identified for it on the page. It is a lead toward, not evidence of, a Douglas–Ross intimacy.
**FRYER ASSERTS NO DOUGLAS–ROSS AFFAIR.** Searched the full OCR text layer for the claim ("affair with Bosie", "Robbie and Bosie", "Bosie and Robbie", "lover", "seduc", "in love with"): the book has no statement, hint or discussion of a sexual or romantic relationship between the two. Fryer's account of their connection is co-membership of Wilde's circle (1892 onward), joint exposure to prosecution over the Bruges schoolboy Dansey in 1893 (pp. 78–81), enforced proximity in 1895, and the long feud. This is the **second** named biographer to be read at page whose silence is informative, after Murray.

# ROSS — FREDDIE SMITH (not on the roster; new-person candidate)

**Passage F7 — the meeting, the ages, the cover of employment (printed 177)**

> Not that Robbie himself intended to remain exclusively emotionally bound to the late playwright.   He had had plenty of sexual adventures, both during Oscar's lifetime and since.   And early in 1903, he met a young man with whom he entered into a relationship quite unlike his other brief encounters with boys.   Frederick (Freddie) Smith was an attractive, unpretentious, eighteen-year-old clerk; not someone of Robbie's class or intellectual capacity, but nonetheless keen to learn and improve himself.

and, contiguously:

> The fact that Robbie was almost twice his age does not seem to have particularly bothered either of them; indeed, theirs was almost a mirror-image of the generational gap between Oscar and Robbie when they had first known each other.   But as the relationship with Freddie developed – soon cloaked by the superficial respectability of Freddie's employment as Robbie's secretary – it became a matter for gossip and for family concern.

— Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 177.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 199, printed folio **177** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact (two contiguous paragraphs of one passage, quoted in sequence)
**Supports:** Fryer treats Freddie Smith as the central romantic relationship of Ross's later life. Ages stated exactly as Fryer states them: Smith eighteen in early 1903; Ross "almost twice his age" (Ross b. 1869, so 33–34). **No mention of George Ives anywhere in this passage or the book** — see the Freddie Smith / Cook note at the foot of this dossier.

**Passage F8 — moving in together, 15 Vicarage Gardens (printed 192)**

> Astonishingly, Robbie reacted to this latest example of Edwardian England's homophobia by defiantly inviting his young secretary-companion Freddie Smith to move in with him in a charming house, on which he had taken a short lease, at 15 Vicarage Gardens, Kensington, just a few minutes walk from his previous rooms in Hornton Street.   Robbie's remaining family were horrified by news of this arrangement, and deputed his nephew Edward Jones, who was only seven years his junior, to remonstrate with him.

— Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 192.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 214, printed folio **192** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Documented cohabitation, dated by context to 1906 (immediately after Christopher Millard's arrest and conviction, narrated in the preceding paragraph on the same page). Ross moves out of the Hornton Street rooms he shared with Adey and into a house with Smith — the pivot from the Adey household to the Smith household.

**Passage F9 — "his lover", and the age (printed 193)**

> Though he never flaunted his homosexuality in public, Robbie saw no reason to be ashamed of his relationship with Freddie, who had not yet turned twenty.   Moreover, he was becoming increasingly proud of his young protégé, who had developed into a talented amateur actor.   He used his second name Stanley on stage.   Shamelessly, Robbie took advantage of his journalistic outlets to promote his lover's acting career, writing a glowing review in the _Academy_ of a Bijou Theatre production of the Welsh poet Arthur Symons's morality play _The Fool of the World_.

— Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 193.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 215, printed folio **193** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Fryer's word for Smith is "his lover", stated flatly and without hedge. Smith performed as "F. Stanley Smith" / "Mr Stanley Smith" (Ross's own _Academy_ review is block-quoted on the same page).


**Passage F10 — the end of it, autumn 1913 (printed 230)**

> On the home front, he had decided to terminate his relationship with Freddie Smith, who had failed to give him the sort of loving support he had needed during his recent troubles.   Although he knew Freddie would feign enthusiasm at his return, he was conscious that the passion had gone out of their union, and that more disappointment and heartache would lie ahead if he did not bring things to an end.

- Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 230.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 252, printed folio **230** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 - verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** End of the relationship, dated by context to Ross's return to London on 5 October 1913 from his Russian journey, during the Douglas/Crosland threats. Fryer's word for it is "their union". Ross had Christopher Millard withhold the date of his return so Smith would not be at home.
**Interval:** early 1903 (F7) to autumn 1913 (F10) - about a decade, and the longest documented romantic relationship of Ross's life. Still flourishing c. 1911 at 13 Lower Grosvenor Gardens, "more balanced" once Smith inherited money and stopped working as Ross's secretary (printed 214, PDF 236, folio confirmed; same page calls Ross and Douglas "Oscar Wilde's lovers" - i.e. each Wilde's, not each other's).

**Passage F11 — sworn testimony at the 1914 Old Bailey trial (printed 238)**

> Thirteen other witnesses were produced in Bosie's defence, including a young woman who had taken part in amateur dramatics with Freddie Smith.   She testified that she had seen Robbie hug Freddie and call him 'my darling'.   A priest, whom Freddie had served as an acolyte, spoke of his revulsion when he had seen Robbie's lover with make-up on his face.

- Fryer, _Robbie Ross_ (2000), p. 238.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 260, printed folio **238** confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 - verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** The relationship entering the criminal record. Douglas's plea of justification (printed 237, PDF 259, folio confirmed) cited "four cases of alleged gross indecency: with Biscoe Wortham's Bruges pupil, Dansey, two decades before, Freddie Smith, Charlie Garratt and a boy called William Edwards"; the trial opened at the Old Bailey on 19 November [1914]. **This is the shape the taxonomy revision was written for:** the surviving third-party attestation of the Ross-Smith relationship is testimony gathered by a hostile ex-friend for a prosecution. Fryer spells the Bruges pupil **Dansey**; Murray (2020) spells him **Danney** - same boy, note the variance.

# FREDDIE SMITH - and what Fryer does **not** say

Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (printed 100), says Freddie Smith left George Ives's Adelaide Road household "to move in with Robbie Ross", citing nothing; the claim is already recorded as a `context_engagement` on `ives.json` with a strong caveat. **Fryer independently and abundantly documents the Ross end of it** - the meeting in early 1903, the secretaryship as cover, the move into 15 Vicarage Gardens, "his lover", the decade, the 1913 break, the 1914 trial - so Smith is now a very strong new-person candidate (index entry: Smith, Frederick (Freddie), printed 177, 192-3, 200, 214, 224, 230, 237, 238).

**But Fryer corroborates none of the Ives half.** He gives Smith's provenance as an "eighteen-year-old clerk" whom Ross "met" early in 1903, and never mentions Ives in connection with him - nor anywhere near him. So the two books do not cross-check each other: Cook supplies the Ives household and the departure; Fryer supplies everything about the relationship with Ross. The bridge between the Ives circle and the Wilde circle still rests on Cook's single uncited sentence. Recording that as an open question rather than a corroboration.

Also note the **date friction**: Cook has Smith in the Adelaide Road household "from 1905", leaving for Ross at an undated later point; Fryer has Ross and Smith together from early 1903 and cohabiting from 1906. The two accounts can be reconciled only loosely, and neither author knows about the other's.
