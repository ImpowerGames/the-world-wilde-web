## douglas — nights at the Albany, c. 1893-1895

**Passage 1 — Cook's flat statement**

> However, although there were increasing suspicions about these figures and domestic arrangements during the period, they continued to provide a relatively secure screen for the sexual and social lives of wealthy men in London. Ives slept with Lord Alfred Douglas at the Albany, and he also enjoyed uninterrupted meals there, at his former chambers in St James', and at nearby clubs with Wilde, Edward Carpenter, Robert Ross, Oscar Browning and others.

— Matt Cook, _London and the Culture of Homosexuality, 1885-1914_ (2003), p. 32.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 48 (printed folio 32 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Ives and Douglas slept together at Ives's Albany chambers (Cook's assertion, resting on the Ives diary — his n. 154).

**Passage 2 — the night and the refused third man**

> Ives shared his new home, and sometimes his bed, with James Goddard (Kit), the son of one of his father's employees, and Harold Holt (Cubby), his grandmother's former secretary. It was also here that he spent the night with Lord Alfred Douglas, though he refused to allow a third party to join them because he 'thought it wouldn't do in the Albany'. The episode reveals Ives' enduring sense of propriety and reservation. He could not embrace Douglas' more abandoned lifestyle and worried about the consequences of the young lord's indiscretions.

— Cook (2003), p. 146.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 162 (printed folio 146 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** the class-deciding scene: Douglas spent the night at E4 the Albany; Ives refused a third man, quoting the diary phrase 'thought it wouldn't do in the Albany'. Note Cook gives no date for the night; "his new home" = E4 the Albany, occupied from 30 June 1894 (see Letters p. 592 n. 3 below). The repo owner's paraphrase "it wouldn't do in the Albany" matches Cook's quoted fragment almost exactly (Cook has 'thought it wouldn't do in the Albany').

**Passage 3 — Ives's diary on Douglas's recklessness (quoted by Cook)**

> 'I warned Lord A more than once that he was indulging in homosexuality to a reckless and highly dangerous degree. For tho' I had no objection to the thing itself we were all afraid he would get arrested any day.'

— Ives diary, quoted in Cook (2003), pp. 146-7 (sentence breaks across the page turn).
**Provenance:** repo PDF pages 162-163 (printed folios 146-147 confirmed on rendered pages), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Ives's own voice on Douglas's conduct — "no objection to the thing itself" — and the circle's fear of arrest. Undated by Cook.

**Passage 4 — the 'traitor' verdict**

> This concern evaporated when Douglas turned on Wilde: Ives added the words 'traitor' to any mention of him in the diary.

— Cook (2003), p. 147.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 163 (printed folio 147 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** the rupture; terminus of the Ives-Douglas attachment (undated by Cook).

**Passage 5 — Wilde brings them together, October 1893 (primary)**

> To George Ives
> MS Clark
>
> [Circa 12 October 1893]  10 & 11 St James's Place, SW
> So sorry you could not come to luncheon: will you dine at the Savoy on Sunday at 8 o'clock? Alfred Douglas and Egbert Sebright are coming. Do come. Yours
> OSCAR WILDE

— Wilde to Ives, *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 573.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 625 (printed folio 573 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** earliest dated documentary contact between Ives and Douglas in sources read: Wilde's dinner invitation naming Douglas.

**Passage 5a — editorial note dating the dinner**

> The dinner, which took place on Sunday, 15 October, was recorded by Ives in his diary the following day. Sir Egbert Sebright, tenth baronet (1871-97), did not appear.

— Holland & Hart-Davis, editorial note, *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 573 n. 5.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 625 (printed folio 573 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** the dinner happened 15 October 1893 and is in Ives's diary.

**Passage 6 — 'O and B came to tea', July 1894 (editorial note on the Albany)**

> Ives had moved into E4 Albany in Piccadilly on 30 June. According to his diary 'O[scar] and B[osie] came to tea' on 5 July.

— Holland & Hart-Davis, editorial note, *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 592 n. 3 (note to Wilde's letter to Ives of [circa 30 June 1894]).
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 644 (printed folio 592 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** E4 Albany move-in date 30 June 1894; Douglas ("B[osie]") visiting Ives's Albany rooms 5 July 1894 per the diary. Constrains the Albany night(s): they postdate 30 June 1894 — the repo hypothesis "around October 1893" cannot be right for the *Albany* nights specifically (Oct 1893 is when Wilde introduced them at dinner).

**Pointer (unverified):** Kaplan, *Sodom on the Thames* (2005) reportedly prints the Ives diary passages on Douglas at the Albany at fuller length. No repo copy; not read this session. Recorded in `douglas--ives.json` as a pointer.

**Rejected candidates:** Cook p. 31 "In 1894 Ives took chambers at The Albany (Flat E4), off Piccadilly" — kept for ives.json bio, redundant to Passage 6's sharper date for the relationship file. (Small conflict: Cook p. 31 says "In 1894 Ives took chambers"; Cook p. 145 "in 1894 moved into Flat E4"; Letters n. 3 gives the exact day 30 June 1894 — no real conflict, Letters is sharper.)

## wilde — the passionate kiss; friendship to the end

**Passage 1 — the kiss (class-deciding)**

> Ives' social circuit expanded when he met Wilde shortly after his move to St James'. London, he said in October 1891, was a 'grand place', and he enjoyed the company of Wilde and his circle in some of the new continental cafés and at the Authors' Club, the Lyric Club and the New Travellers' Club, where Wilde once kissed him 'passionately' goodbye.

— Cook (2003), p. 145. (The word 'passionately' is Cook quoting the Ives diary; Cook's n. 12.)
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 161 (printed folio 145 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** the single documented passionate kiss, at the New Travellers' Club; undated by Cook (early-1890s London social round); also Cook's dating of the first meeting ("shortly after" Ives's July 1891 move to 56 St James' Street — cf. p. 145 above it; Lewis podcast says "in about 1893").

**Passage 2 — Wilde from prison, 1896**

> Please remember me very kindly to George Ives. I was greatly touched at hearing of his desire to come and see me. In the terrible solitude and silence in which one lives a message or a memory means a great deal. I hope he is hard at work writing books. I am very glad you know him. He is such a good fellow and so clever.

— Wilde to R. H. Sherard, Wednesday [26 August 1896], from HM Prison, Reading; *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 662.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 713 (printed folio 662 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** warmth of the bond through the prison years; Ives wished to visit Wilde in prison.

**Passage 2a — editorial note: Ives's poems for Wilde**

> Ives published *A Book of Chains* anonymously in 1897, which contained poems bearing directly on his feelings for Wilde in prison.

— Holland & Hart-Davis, editorial note, *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 662 n. 2.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 713 (printed folio 662 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Ives's feelings for Wilde had a poetic record (editorial characterization).

**Passage 3 — Wilde on Ives's caution, 1897**

> George Ives has sent me his poems - of course without an inscription. His caution is amusing. He means well, which is the worst of it.

— Wilde to Robert Ross, Tuesday, 16 November 1897, from Villa Giudice, Posilippo; *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 980.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 1028 (printed folio 980 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** texture — Wilde's affectionate exasperation at Ives's secrecy (the poems are *A Book of Chains*, per the note's cross-reference "See note 2, p.662").

**Passage 4 — 'silly mania for secrecy' / 'a great baby', 1900**

> Do, when again, if ever, you come to Paris let me know, and also let me know your address. Don't have with *me* the silly mania for secrecy that makes you miss the value of things: to you it is of more importance to conceal your address from a friend, than to see your friend. … On the whole, George, you are a great baby. One can't help being angry with you. Ever yours
> OSCAR

— Wilde to Ives, 12 February [Postmark 1900], Hôtel d'Alsace; *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 1172.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 1218 (printed folio 1172 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision (mid-letter paragraphs elided with spaced ellipsis)
**Supports:** the famous "silly mania for secrecy" line is Wilde to Ives, Feb 1900; intimacy and friction of the late friendship.

**Passage 5 — last letters, September 1900**

> I hope you will come over soon, and not alone: but don't conceal your charming friends: you usually hide them from me.

— Wilde to Ives, Saturday [8 September 1900] [Postmark 9 September 1900], Paris; *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 1197.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 1243 (printed folio 1197 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** friendship continuous to within three months of Wilde's death; Wilde teasing Ives's compartmentalized love life.

## bloodworth — the Adelaide Road household, 1909-1950

**Passage 1 — the household (class-relevant, quoted in full context)**

> At the close of the 1890s Ives moved from Piccadilly; first to his former home in Park Road and then, in 1905, to Adelaide Road in Primrose Hill. Adelaide Road was, according to historian Donald Olsen, 'the essence of suburbia', and in the diary for the post-1905 period there is a sense of Ives settling down - not to a 'conventional' family, but with a series of working- and lower-middle-class men whom he paternalistically referred to as his 'children'. Kit brought his wife and children to live there for a while, and in 1909, Harold Bloodworth, a teenage footballer, moved in and stayed until Ives' death in 1950.

— Cook (2003), p. 148.
**Provenance:** repo PDF page 164 (printed folio 148 confirmed on rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Bloodworth's entry into the household (1909, "a teenage footballer") and its duration (to 1950); the household's character ("not… a 'conventional' family"; the 'children' framing). **Cook stops short of calling Bloodworth a lover** — the sentence that follows is about seclusion ("Ives' aim at Adelaide Road was seclusion for himself and his housemates"), and the paragraph closes calling it "his unconventional household".

**Passage 2 — Lewis on the household (spoken word, ASR transcript — quote with caveat)**

> He moved from the countryside into London, lived in London for most of his life, mostly in a suburban house in North London, where he lived with a succession of younger men who sometimes stayed for just a few years, sometimes they stayed for decades.

— Brian Lewis, interviewed on *Discovery & Inspiration* (National Humanities Center podcast), repo ASR transcript.
**Provenance:** repo file `docs/research/queer-history/victorian/ives/audio/Discovery & Inspiration - Brian Lewis, "George Cecil Ives and the Transformation of Discourses on Sexuality".txt`, read 2026-08-03. ASR transcript — wording may vary slightly from the audio; known ASR garbles elsewhere in the file ("Order of Carinia/Carolinia" = Chaeronea, "Geoffrey Weeks" = Jeffrey Weeks). · verification: verified-exact (against the repo transcript, not the audio)
**Supports:** the household pattern ("a succession of younger men… sometimes they stayed for decades" — Bloodworth is the stayed-for-decades case, though Lewis names no one here). Lewis also says (same transcript): "All of these domestic relationships are an important part of queer history…" and, on the Greek pederastic model, "Although Ives' relationships were all with adults" — relevant nuance against over-reading "teenage footballer".

**Classification note (proposal):** alleged, with a `disputed` block. The lover characterization for Bloodworth specifically is asserted in the repo owner's research leads (Lewis interview prep: Ives's "children" glossed as his lovers, Bloodworth = "Pug") but no citable source read this session states it; Cook documents a forty-one-year household membership without sexual characterization. The Ives diaries (HRC) are where the question resolves — pointer left.

## Person-facts log (for the people JSONs)

- **Born/died 1867-1950:** Letters p. 573 n. 3 ("George Cecil Ives (1867-1950)"), PDF 625, folio confirmed. Podcast host intro concurs ("Born in 1867"; died "in the mid-20th century"; Lewis: "when he died in 1950"). Cook p. 148 ("until Ives' death in 1950").
- **Parentage:** Cook p. 143 (PDF 159, folio confirmed): "He was the illegitimate son of Gordon Maynard Ives and the Baroness de Molarti of Spain, and was brought up by his paternal grandmother, the Hon. Emma Ives." Letters n. 3 names the father "Colonel Gordon Maynard Gordon-Ives CB (1837-1907)". Lewis podcast: the Spanish-aristocracy maternal descent "not borne out by the actual census records".
- **Diary:** Cook p. 143: "His diary covers the period from 1886 to 1950 and runs to 122 volumes and around three million words." Began at nineteen, dividing time "largely between Magdalene College, Cambridge, and his 'den' at his grandmother's London home in Park Road" (Cook p. 144, PDF 160, folio confirmed). Archive "split between Yale and the University of Texas at Austin" (Cook p. 143); the 45-volume scrapbook/casebook (Cook p. 143).
- **Order of Chaeronea:** Letters p. 573 n. 3: founded "in about 1893… in memory of the young band of Thebans slaughtered by the Macedonians in 338 BC" (spelled 'Chaeronea' there); Cook pp. 32, 143 spells it "the Order of the Chaerona" and calls it "the first group of its kind in Britain" (p. 143), formed "in the early 1890s" (p. 32).
- **Albany:** chambers at 56 St James' Street from July 1891 (Cook p. 145); Flat E4 the Albany from 30 June 1894 (Letters p. 592 n. 3; Cook pp. 31, 145 say "1894"); left Piccadilly "at the close of the 1890s" (Cook p. 148).
- **Adelaide Road, Primrose Hill from 1905** (Cook p. 148); household of "working- and lower-middle-class men whom he paternalistically referred to as his 'children'" (Cook p. 148).
- **The Humanitarian article, October 1894** (Cook p. 147; Wilde's letter p. 619 + n. 2 — the *Review of Reviews* attack).
- **Off-roster household intimates (context_engagements):** James Goddard ("Kit") and Harold Holt ("Cubby") — "Ives shared his new home, and sometimes his bed, with James Goddard (Kit), the son of one of his father's employees, and Harold Holt (Cubby), his grandmother's former secretary." (Cook p. 146, PDF 162, folio confirmed.) Kit later "brought his wife and children to live" at Adelaide Road (Cook p. 148).
