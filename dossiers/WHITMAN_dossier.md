**Passage 1 — Wilde's letter to Whitman (the one letter in the Letters)**

> To Walt Whitman
> MS Congress
>
> [Postmark 1 March 1882] [Postmark Chicago]
> My dear dear Walt, Swinburne has just written to me to say as follows:
>
> 'I am sincerely interested and gratified by your account of Walt Whitman and the assurance of his kindly and friendly feeling towards me: and I thank you, no less sincerely, for your kindness in sending me word of it. … I have by no manner of means [either 'forgotten him' or] relaxed my admiration of his noblest work – such parts, above all, of his writings, as treat of the noblest subjects, material and spiritual, with which poetry can deal. I have always thought it, and I believe it will hereafter be generally thought, his highest and surely most enviable distinction that he never speaks so well as when he speaks of great matters – liberty, for instance, and death. …'
>
> There! You see how you remain in our hearts, and how simply and grandly Swinburne speaks of you, knowing you to be simple and grand yourself.
> Will you in return send me for Swinburne a copy of your *Essay on Poetry* – the pamphlet – with your name and his on it: it would please him so much.
> Before I leave America I must see you again. There is no one in this wide great world of America whom I love and honour so much.
> With warm affection, and honourable admiration OSCAR WILDE

— Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), pp. 144-5.
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 167-168, printed folios 144 and 145 confirmed on rendered pages, read as page images 2026-08-03. Offset here is +23 (NOT the +22 the early-range note suggested). Elisions: two clauses inside the quoted Swinburne letter (one containing a phrase I could not read with confidence at this render size); all Wilde-voice text is transcribed in full. Square brackets around 'either forgotten him or' are the edition's own (phrases Wilde added when copying Swinburne).
**Supports:** Wilde-side warmth after the January 1882 Camden visit: "Before I leave America I must see you again. There is no one in this wide great world of America whom I love and honour so much." Postmark gives the date anchor 1 March 1882 and implies an intended second visit.

**Passage 2 — editorial footnote with Whitman's particulars and the first visit date**

> The great American poet (1819–92) was now living in Camden, New Jersey, a suburb of Philadelphia across the Delaware, where Wilde had been taken by Stoddart to see him on 18 January. Although his *Leaves of Grass* had been published as long ago as 1855, the eighth edition was withdrawn by its publishers in 1882 on a threatened prosecution for indecency. Whitman's prose work, *Specimen Days and Collect*, was published later in 1882.

— Holland & Hart-Davis, editorial note, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 144 n. 1.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 167, printed folio 144 confirmed on rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03.
**Supports:** Whitman b. 1819 d. 1892; residence Camden NJ; first visit 18 January [1882], Wilde taken by Stoddart.

— Wilde to J. M. Stoddart, [Circa 1 March 1882], _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 145.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 168, printed folio 145 confirmed on rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03.

**Passage 4 — the visit was at Whitman's invitation (letter to Mrs George Lewis, complete)**

> To Mrs George Lewis
> MS Rosenbach
>
> [17 January 1882] Philadelphia
> My dear Mrs Lewis, Everything is going on brilliantly. I send you papers regularly. Of course they are full of lies, but you will not take them seriously. I go to see Walt Whitman, at his invitation, tomorrow. Tonight I lecture to 1500 people. Best regards to Mr Lewis and Mr Burne-Jones. Truly yours OSCAR WILDE

— Wilde, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 128.

— Wilde to J. M. Stoddart, 17 April [1882], Kansas City, _Complete Letters_ (2000), p. 163. Editorial n. 2 on the same page: "Walt Whitman lived in Camden, New Jersey."

**Passage 6 — "I never completely make Wilde out" (vol. 2, folio 192 in stream; entry of ~12-13 July 1888)**

> I asked W. if he had ever had any correspondence with Oscar Wilde? "No correspondence, though he has written me letters. Did I never show you a fine letter I had from him while he was making his American trip? No? Well—it is about here in the mess—somewhere about, God knows where: if it ever shows its head I'll grab it—you may have it. I never completely make Wilde out—out for good or bad. He writes exquisitely—is as lucid as a star on a clear night—but there seems to be a little substance lacking at the root—something—what is it? I have no sympathy with the crowd of the scorners who want to crowd him off the earth."

— Traubel, _With Walt Whitman in Camden_, vol. 2, p. 192 (folio "191" appears just above in the stream; index gives 192).
**Provenance:** djvu.txt of IA `withwaltwhitmani0002unse_c4l4`, lines ~9622-9633, downloaded 2026-08-03 · verification: unverified (djvu OCR; folio from stream + index).
**Supports:** Whitman kept Wilde's letter six years and defends him against "the scorners"; ambivalence about substance, warmth about the man.

**Passage 7 — "was not all fraud … wholly sincere" (vol. 2, folio 279; entry of ~7 Sept. 1888)**

> W. in mighty good feather this evening. Said he had found an Oscar Wilde letter but "would not give it to" me "just yet." Wanted "to read it again." "Wilde," he said, "may have been some of him fraud at that time but was not all fraud. My letter from him seems wholly sincere. He has extraordinary brilliancy of genius with perhaps rather too little root in eternal soils. Wilde gives up too much to the extrinsic decorative values in art."

— Traubel, _With Walt Whitman in Camden_, vol. 2, p. 279.
**Provenance:** djvu.txt of IA `withwaltwhitmani0002unse_c4l4`, lines ~13843-13849, folio "279" in stream below passage, downloaded 2026-08-03 · verification: unverified.
**Supports:** Whitman re-reading Wilde's 1882 letter in 1888; "My letter from him seems wholly sincere."

**Passage 8 — the handover: "both of us friends according each other a mutual respect" (vol. 2, folios 287-289; entry of Sunday 9 Sept. 1888)**

> W. gave me the Wilde letter. I thought he might say more about it. He said little: only this: "It seems all straight and honest to me. I have been told a thousand times what Wilde is but I do not see why Wilde is not what he is and I am not what I am with both of us friends according each other a mutual respect. There is no parade in this note: it wears the simplest clothes—has no sunflower in its button hole—has in fact a cast of virgin simplicity, sincerity. Read it for yourself: see if the letter does not bear me out." He said nothing while I read. He had endorsed the envelope in blue pencil: "from Oscar Wilde early in '82." The postmark was Chicago, March 1, The letter was written in New York.
>
> 1267 Broadway, New York.
> My Dear Dear Walt—Swinburne has just written to me to say as follows. …
>
> [letter text runs to folio 288, signed "Oscar Wilde"; then:] When I looked up after reading the letter W. asked: "Am I not right? Does he strike a false note? It all rings sound and true [p. 289:] to me there. Everybody's been so in the habit of looking at Wilde cross-eyed, sort of, that they have charged the defect of their vision up against Wilde as a weakness in his character."

— Traubel, _With Walt Whitman in Camden_, vol. 2, pp. 287-9.
**Provenance:** djvu.txt of IA `withwaltwhitmani0002unse_c4l4`, lines ~14233-14260 and ~14293-14297 (the two blocks are separated in the stream by the plate facsimile of the letter), folios "287", "288" in stream, downloaded 2026-08-03 · verification: unverified.
**Supports:** Whitman's own framing of the bond: "both of us friends according each other a mutual respect"; he preserved the letter and its envelope, endorsed in his hand. Traubel's print of the letter is the same letter as Complete Letters pp. 144-5 (which prints it from MS Congress — i.e. this very Feinberg/Traubel-descended original; Traubel gives the address 1267 Broadway, New York, postmark Chicago March 1).

**Passage 9 — Whitman still citing "Oscar Wilde's Boston declaration" (vol. 3, folios 11 and 276; Nov. 1888 - Jan. 1889)**

> … I recall a little story told of Oscar Wilde when he was in this country — in Boston, at some drawing room reception. Wilde said to those there — said it gravely, I think — (at least, I have taken it gravely): 'If I may presume to speak for them — to include myself among them — I should say, it is not your praise, your laudations, that we, the poets, seek, but your comprehension — your recognition of what we stand for and what we effect.'

— Traubel, _With Walt Whitman in Camden_, vol. 3, p. 11; and at p. 276: "I still stick by Oscar Wilde's Boston declaration: I still say, it is not your applause, flattery, acceptance even, we seek: but we seek to be understood …"
**Provenance:** djvu.txt of IA `withwaltwhitmani0003unse`, lines ~1014-1022 and ~13488-13494, folios "11" and "276" in stream, downloaded 2026-08-03 · verification: unverified.
**Supports:** Wilde's words stayed with Whitman as a touchstone into 1889 — color for the lasting impression, not class-deciding.

**Passage 11 — "Wilde and Whitman", The Philadelphia Press, 19 January 1882 (Whitman Archive med.00534)**

> "Yes, Mr. Wilde came to see me early this afternoon," said Walt, "and I took him up to my den, where we had a jolly good time. I think he was glad to get away from lecturing, and fashionable society, and spend a time with an 'old rough.' We had a very happy time together. I think him genuine, honest and manly. I was glad to have him with me, for his youthful health, enthusiasm and buoyancy are refreshing. …"
>
> Thus talking, Walt Whitman led the way to his "den," as he calls it, on the third floor. "Wilde and I drank a bottle of wine down stairs," he continued, "and when we came up here, where we could be on 'thee and thou' terms, one of the first things I said was that I should call him 'Oscar;' 'I like that so much,' he answered, laying his hand on my knee. He seemed to me like a great big, splendid boy," said Whitman, stroking his silvery beard. "He is so frank and outspoken, and manly. I don't see why such mocking things are written of him. He has the English society drawl, but his enunciation is better than I ever heard in a young Englishman or Irishman before. We talked here for two hours. I said to him: 'Oscar, you must be thirsty. I'll make you some punch.' 'Yes, I am thirsty,' he acknowledged, and I did make him a big glass of milk punch, and he tossed it off, and away he went."
>
> … As the æsthete departed, Mr. Whitman's farewell was: "Good bye, Oscar, God bless you."
>
> During the ride over from Camden, in company with J. M. Stoddart, the publisher, Oscar Wilde was very silent, and seemed deeply affected by the interview. He spoke admiringly of "the grand old man," and of his struggles and triumphs.

— Anonymous, "Wilde and Whitman," *The Philadelphia Press*, 19 January 1882; Whitman Archive ID med.00534 ("Our transcription is based on a digital image of a microfilm copy of an original issue.").
**Provenance:** Wayback capture 20260113032354 of https://whitmanarchive.org/item/med.00534, page text extracted and read 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision (against the Archive's transcription; microfilm image not read) · how read: web.
**Supports:** Whitman's own next-day account of the first visit: invitation-warmth, wine, the den, "thee and thou" terms, first-naming, the hand on the knee, "a great big, splendid boy", the blessing at parting; Wilde "deeply affected" on the ride back.
**Also on the page (not distilled):** Wilde's declaration "I have come to you as to one with whom I have been acquainted almost from the cradle" (Lady Wilde bought *Leaves* "some sixteen years ago"); Whitman's advice "I wish well to you, Oscar … I say 'go ahead'"; the beauty exchange ("all beauty comes from beautiful blood and a beautiful brain"); Whitman's "Good bye, Oscar, God bless you."

### The kiss line — printed home found and read this session

**Passage 12 — Ellmann, "Oscar Meets Walt", NYRB, 3 December 1987 (open on nybooks.com)**

> Wilde copied out Swinburne's letter, omitting only a few words that slightly lowered its effect, and sent it on to "My dear dear Walt" on March 1. He promised to see Whitman again, and did so early in May. This time Stoddart was not present, and the two could talk more freely. Their conversation has not survived, but their parting has. Wilde would later tell George Ives, a proselytizer for sexual deviation in the Nineties, that Whitman had made no effort to conceal his homosexuality from him, as he would do with John Addington Symonds. "The kiss of Walt Whitman," Wilde said, "is still on my lips." [8] He would expand upon this theme a little later when signing John Boyle O'Reilly's autograph book in Boston. Under an inscription by Whitman Wilde wrote of him, "That Spirit which living blamelessly yet dared to kiss the smitten mouth of his own century." (He was quoting lines he had applied to Wordsworth in his poem "Humanitad.") [9]

— Richard Ellmann, "Oscar Meets Walt," *The New York Review of Books*, December 3, 1987 issue (article free on nybooks.com; pre-print of material from his 1987/88 *Oscar Wilde* biography).
