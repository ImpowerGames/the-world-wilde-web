## wilde — Berneval visit, 1897: Wilde's "two loves" declaration; Fothergill declines and drops the correspondence

Proposed class: `propositioned-declined`, `direction: wilde`. All passages below are Sox's narration built on Fothergill's memoirs (previously unpublished, per Sox's introduction); where Sox sets off Fothergill's first-person text, the chain is "Fothergill memoir, as printed in Sox 1991, p. N".

**Passage 1 — the acquaintance and the 1897 frame**

> When Fothergill was 19 he had met Oscar Wilde through Robbie Ross and Reggie Turner. In 1897, he was 21 and came into his inheritance. That year Wilde was released from prison and the following year Augustus John completed his study at the Slade School of Art.

— Sox, _Bachelors of Art_ (1991), p. 137 (closing paragraph of ch. "Rather a Lack of Care").
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 153 (printed folio 137 confirmed), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** met at 19 via Ross/Turner; 21 with inheritance in 1897, the release year. (Sox states ages, not a birth year, here.)

**Passage 2 — Fothergill in the Ross circle; his own confessed wish**

> He put up with Blomfield's for a while and then 'wandered about again, by this time in the company of homosexualists whom I got to know at Robbie Ross's house . . . I did my best to play the part and keep up with their affectations. I found it silly enough. Oscar Wilde was in prison and they were without a leader. . . From their charming and witty behaviour it was difficult to know if they practised the art of Greek love.' Fothergill could not hold his own in their endless gossiping and games, but he knew they were attracted to him because of his 'good looks and youth'.

> Fothergill – as usual – was looking for something but could not find what it was, or, as we will see, perhaps was afraid to commit himself: 'I had all the time a secret longing for a perfect friend, a thing which I couldn't define – something like a gentleman protector – someone who would give me support, direction and place, for I had no thought of helping myself or how I could, but went on complacently spending my capital.'

— Sox, _Bachelors of Art_ (1991), pp. 138–139 (ch. "Two Loves Have I"; second passage runs over the page break).
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 154–155 (printed folios 138–139 confirmed), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** the milieu that produced the visit; Fothergill's own "gentleman protector" longing — the closest thing in Sox to the roster-owner's "prospective kept man" framing, and it is Fothergill's wish, not an arrangement Sox attributes to Ross or Wilde.

**Passage 3 — the visit is documented in Wilde's letters; dating hedge**

> Fothergill's visit has gone heretofore unrecorded although Rupert Hart-Davis's edition of selected letters of Oscar Wilde contains veiled references to it: 1 June 1897, Wilde wrote to Ross that he wanted to continue to live at Berneval and 'send over the architect [Fothergill]'. On 5 June, Wilde described the rooms at the Hotel de la Plage and what expenses might be expected for visitors like Ross and Reggie, but 'Architects, on the other hand, are taken at a reduction. I have special terms for architects.' On 20 July, again to Ross, Wilde said he 'expected Ross on August the First: also, the architect'.

> We do not know exactly when Fothergill appeared and from his memoirs it seems he visited Wilde alone. … on 21 September, Wilde wrote to Ross that despite several visits by friends to Berneval he was so lonely that he was on the brink of killing himself: in the last month there, Reggie Turner had given him three days, and 'Rowland a sextette of suns'.

— Sox, _Bachelors of Art_ (1991), p. 139. Sox's square-bracketed "[Fothergill]" gloss and his endnote markers (nn. 7–11) are in the original; "Rowland" = Fothergill (called Rowland by some friends, Roland or Ronald by others — Sox p. 133, verified at-page).
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 155 (printed folio 139 confirmed), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision (intervening Naples sentence elided in second block; full on page)
**Supports:** visit at Berneval, summer 1897; window bounded by Wilde's letters (1 June anticipation → 21 Sept "sextette of suns" = six days in his last month there); Sox's explicit hedge on the exact date; visited alone.

**Passage 4 — arrival (Fothergill memoir, first person)**

> From the railings of the boat, as she got to Dieppe harbour, I looked down upon what must be a great man standing alone and looking up. I had seen a photograph of this beautiful looking man and here was a huge and fat person in white flannels with a comical little red beret on top of it all. Rather vulgar I thought. My heart sank, but his sweet kindness put me right quickly.

— Fothergill memoir, as printed in Sox 1991, pp. 139–140 (set-off block, "Fothergill described his arrival at Dieppe:").
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 155–156 (printed folios 139–140 confirmed), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact (block continues on p. 140 with the cathedral, the cab to the Chalet Bourgeat, Wordsworth, and the shared café expenses — "sharing expenses when I should have paid all and not only 35/-a week for Berneval. But I didn't know he was so hard up.")
**Supports:** the meeting happened, first-person; Wilde met his boat at Dieppe; Fothergill stayed at the Chalet Bourgeat (Sox's bracketed note on p. 140: Wilde had rented the Chalet Bourgeat from 15 June).

**Passage 5 — THE class-deciding passage: the declaration and Fothergill's response**

> Both Smithers and Fothergill knew Wilde was troubled about Bosie Douglas who was either unwilling to see him or prevented by Ross. Fothergill said Wilde liked him and declared to him: 'Two loves have I: the one of comfort; the other of despair. The one has black; the other golden hair.' Fothergill commented: 'I don't think I could have given much comfort. I was too concentrated on myself. I've ever been incapable of sympathy, even with this sad thing.'

— Sox, _Bachelors of Art_ (1991), p. 141 (ch. "Two Loves Have I" — the chapter title is this declaration; Wilde is reworking Shakespeare's Sonnet 144, "Two loves I have of comfort and despair" [annotation mine, not Sox's]).
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 157 (printed folio 141 confirmed), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Wilde's declaration casting the dark-haired Fothergill as the love "of comfort" against golden-haired Douglas's "despair"; Fothergill's own retrospective refusal of the role. This, plus the jacket gloss (below), is the "proposition"; Sox narrates no more explicit sexual approach in the pages read.

**Passage 6 — the decline made effective: Fothergill drops him**

> When Fothergill returned home he received 'some charming letters with his lovely handwriting', but admitted that the feeling against Wilde and any knowledge of him was then so strong in England that he decided 'to drop him': 'I actually wrote that it was not "politic" to go on with it. What an awful expression!' Wilde replied saying he didn't know what Fothergill meant by 'politic'. For Fothergill it was finished, but he said he had assimilated Wilde's manner – even his ponderous intonation 'so far as my voice allowed'.

— Sox, _Bachelors of Art_ (1991), pp. 141–142 (runs over the page break at "he").
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 157–158 (printed folios 141–142 confirmed), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** the decline in Fothergill's own written act and words ("not 'politic' to go on with it"); Wilde's puzzled reply; the connection ended by Fothergill.

**Passage 7 — aftermath and regret (context, not edge evidence)**

> Fothergill added a sad footnote to his remembrance of Wilde. After he had taken up with Warren in Rome, Warren was well aware that a year before, Fothergill had had 'friendships with one or two boys'. … Then came what Fothergill called 'the tragedy'. One day he went down the Via del Corso and looking across the road at Aragno's Café he saw Wilde, sitting alone at a table. He stopped and watched him: 'He sat so still, looking at nothing, and had that same, sad friendless face . . . I wondered – should I go over to him or, would it once more be that awful word "politic", and a betrayal of my duty to Ned's work? I had to decide for this. I passed on, and never saw him again, but my tragic picture of it all sickens me still.'

— Sox, _Bachelors of Art_ (1991), p. 142. Immediately after: "That feeling stayed with Fothergill to the end of his life, and both his sons say he regretted his decision not to have approached Wilde in Rome."
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 158 (printed folio 142 confirmed), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision (Warren-moustache sentences elided mid-passage; full on page)
**Supports:** the decline held to Wilde's death; Fothergill's lifelong regret; establishes the later Warren attachment ("taken up with Warren in Rome") in Sox's words.

**Passage B1 — birth, family, name variants, memoir caveats (p. 133, opening of Part V ch. 1)**

> Of all Ned Warren's companions, the best known – in Britain at least – is John Rowland Fothergill. This is because of his latter-day career as innkeeper extraordinaire and the success of his innkeeping diaries published in the 1930s and 1940s. Fothergill was born on 27 February 1876, the son of George Fothergill, of a fairly well-off Westmorland family. His mother died of scarlet fever when he was two days old.

> However, there is Fothergill's unpublished eighty-one-page memoir written in 1954, three years before he died. … Part of the problem has been that he was called Rowland by some of his friends and Roland or Ronald by others. … Fothergill never finished his memoirs and what we have is highly disorganized. There are few dates given and often Fothergill leaps ahead in his recollections to events occurring a decade or two in the future. … There are many gaps in his story as well. Apparently, he did not want to tell us about certain aspects of his life.

— Sox, _Bachelors of Art_ (1991), p. 133. Same page: Sox quotes Michael Holroyd on Fothergill, "a misfit in modern civilisation . . . possessively homosexual"; Ellmann refers to Rowland Fothergill as a recipient (among fourteen others) of _The Ballad of Reading Gaol_.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 149 (printed folio 133 confirmed), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision (sentences between joined with spaced ellipses; full on page)
**Supports:** born 27 Feb. 1876; full name John Rowland Fothergill; Rowland/Roland/Ronald variants (decodes "Rowland" in Wilde's 21 Sept. letter); Sox's own reliability hedges on the memoir — feeds `certainty_reasoning`.

**Passage B2 — death and career summary (Introduction, printed p. viii)**

> On 26 April 1957, John Rowland Fothergill, the one member of the Lewes House brotherhood to become well known in Britain, died in a modest terraced house in Rugby at the age of 81. During his long life Fothergill had dabbled in classical archaeology, put in brief stints studying at the London School of Architecture and the Slade School of Art, and managed the Carfax Gallery. Posterity, however, will remember his latter day career as an eccentric innkeeper and author.

— Sox, _Bachelors of Art_ (1991), p. viii (Introduction).
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 12 (printed folio viii confirmed), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact. (Rendered type reads "Carfax"; the extracted text layer OCRs it "Cartax".)
**Supports:** died 26 April 1957, Rugby, aged 81; Lewes House membership; archaeology/architecture/Slade/Carfax/innkeeper bio spine.
