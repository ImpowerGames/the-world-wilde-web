## Passages

### A. The editors' main-particulars footnote — dates, the 1890 meeting, the Oakley Street visit

> 1. English poet (1867–1900). He had met Wilde in 1890 and with Robert Sherard visited him at Oakley Street in the dark days between his trials.

Holland & Hart-Davis, editorial footnote, `letters-2000` **printed p. 877** (repo PDF 927, folio confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03). Footnote hangs on the first mention of Dowson in the text, in Wilde to Robert Ross of [? 2 June 1897] from Berneval: "Ernest Dowson,¹ Conder,² and Dal Young – what a name – are coming out to dine and sleep: at least I know they dine, but I believe they don't sleep."

This is the source of record for **born 1867 / died 1900**, and it independently confirms two of the three biographical facts the Sox passage asserts (the 1890 meeting; the Oakley Street visit between the trials) — with the addition that Sherard was the other visitor.

### B. Wilde to Dowson, [? 16 June 1897], Café Suisse, Dieppe — the whole letter

The single most important document for this pair, and the origin of two phrases that reach Sox at second hand. `letters-2000` **printed p. 901** (repo PDF 951, folio confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03). Header: `To Ernest Dowson / MS Maguire / [? 16 June 1897] / Café Suisse, Dieppe`.

> My dear Ernest, I arrived safe, under a cold white moon, at one o'clock in the morning. My servant was asleep, so I woke him up and enquired about his early life, which, as I expected, was quite uninteresting.
>
> There is a fatality about our being together that is astounding – or rather quite probable. Had I stayed at Arques I should have given up all hopes of ever separating from you. Why are you so persistently and perversely wonderful?
>
> Do I see you tomorrow? Try to come over. I hope Thompson and Socrates (*his* Socrates) will come. It was delightful meeting 'Varsity men again: I like them both so much. But I intend to send them both to Oxford, especially Thompson.
>
> Come with vine-leaves in your hair.¹
>
> I suppose I shall see you in ten minutes from this. I am looking out for the green costume that goes so well with your dark hyacinth locks.
>
> I decided this morning to take a *Pernod*. The result was marvellous. At 8.30 I was dead. Now I am alive, and all is perfect, except your absence. *Tout à toi*
>
> SEBASTIAN MELMOTH

Footnote 1 (Holland & Hart-Davis, same page): "Like Eilert Lövborg; see Ibsen's *Hedda Gabler*, end of Act II: 'Ten o'clock . . . and back he'll come. I can just see him. With vine leaves in his hair. Flushed and confident.'"

**Note what this settles.** Sox's "Wilde looked for 'the green costume' to go with Dowson's 'dark hyacinth locks'" is not a characterization by a third party — it is Sox quoting **this letter**, i.e. Wilde's own words. The Wilde-voice original is therefore available first-hand at printed p. 901 and the edge does not need Sox for it.

The unfoliated plate section (repo PDF 600; no printed folio) reproduces a photograph of Dowson captioned with a line from passage B:

### D. Wilde to Dowson, [? 5 June 1897], Hotel de la Plage, Berneval-sur-Mer — the first letter

`letters-2000` **printed p. 883** (repo PDF 933, folio confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03). Opening:

> Cher Monsieur le Poète, It was most kind of you coming to see me, and I thank you very sincerely and gratefully for your pleasant companionship and the many gentle ways by which you recalled to me that, once at any rate, I was a Lord of Language, and had myself the soul of a poet.

### H. Wilde to Dowson, Wednesday 6.30 [? 30 June 1897], Café Suisse, Dieppe

Same page — `letters-2000` **printed p. 908**, running over to **909** (repo PDF 958–959, both folios confirmed on the rendered pages, read as page images 2026-08-03).

> Dear Ernest, I write a little line, whose only excuse is its entire illegibility, to tell you how charming you are (at Berneval) and how much I like *your* friend, *and mine*, the dear Achille. He is a most noble and splendid fellow, and I feel happy to have his esteem and friendship.
>
> Tonight I am going to read your poems – your lovely lyrics – words with wings *you* write always. It is an exquisite gift, and fortunately rare in an age whose prose is more poetic than its poetry.
>
> Do come soon and see me.

and, over the page break, the close:

> You and Achille and Achille's friend and I must *all* be at Berneval together. I am making arrangements.
>
> Give to Achille my sincere friendship: you have it, and other things, always. S. M.

The last clause ("you have it, and other things, always") is the only line in the twelve letters that gestures at something withheld from the page, and it is addressed to Dowson while nominally about Achille Fromentin. Recorded here; **not** promoted to the record, because reading it as more than epistolary warmth requires the coding hypothesis and no source read this session does so.

### I. Wilde on Dowson's death — to Leonard Smithers, [circa 24 February 1900], Paris

`letters-2000` **printed p. 1173** (repo PDF 1219, folio confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03). Wilde's last word on Dowson, written the day after the death:

> My dear Smithers, I am greatly distressed to hear of Ernest's death: how sudden it must have been! Poor wounded wonderful fellow that he was, a tragic reproduction of all tragic poetry, like a symbol, or a scene. I hope bay-leaves will be laid on his tomb, and rue, and myrtle too, for he knew what love is.
>
> He was a sweet singer, with a note all the lovelier because it reminds us of how thrushes sang in Shakespeare's day. If he is not yet laid to rest or unrest, do put some flowers for me on his grave.

Editors' footnote 2, same page: "Ernest Dowson died in Robert Sherard's house at Catford on 23 February 1900. He was buried in the Roman Catholic part of Lewisham Cemetery on 27 February." — the source of record for **died 23 February 1900**.

Note the addressee: this is Wilde speaking *about* Dowson to a third party, so it is Wilde's own testimony but not part of the correspondence between them. "he knew what love is" is elegiac and unspecific; Wilde does not say for whom.

## The Sox passage — and the "first hyacinth since Douglas" attribution

### K. Sox on Dowson at the Chalet Bourgeat

`sox-1991` **printed p. 141** (repo PDF 157, folio confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03). Chapter "Two Loves Have I", Part V (Fothergill's story). The setting is established on the facing page: `sox-1991` **printed p. 140** (repo PDF 156, folio confirmed, read as page image 2026-08-03) — "Of the other guests at Chalet Bourgeat at this time, Fothergill mentioned seeing only two: Leonard Smithers … [and] the English poet Ernest Dowson." So this is **Berneval during Fothergill's own visit**, not Oakley Street and not Wilde's Paris hotel; the Oakley Street visit is a separate fact mentioned in passing.

The whole Dowson paragraph, verbatim:

> The other guest was the English poet Ernest Dowson. He had met Wilde in 1890, visiting him at Oakley Street in the dark days between his trials. Dowson was described as Wilde's 'first hyacinth since Douglas: But this flower was wilting, and had not much longer to live'.¹² Despite the wilting, Wilde looked for 'the green costume' which went well with Dowson's 'dark hyacinth locks'. Fothergill commented that Dowson was in love with the owner of the Café Royal, but was 'faithful only after his fashion'. Although Dowson was in the house he did not appear. Wilde said they had quarrelled and Dowson, to spite him, 'had had all his long curls cut'. Apparently, Dowson had also tried to persuade Wilde to try sexual relations with women again. After returning from a Dieppe prostitute, Wilde said to Dowson with distaste: 'The first these ten years, and it shall be the last. It was like chewing cold mutton.' Then as an afterthought: 'But tell it in England, where it will entirely restore my reputation.'¹³

Sox's index (printed p. 282, PDF 314) lists "Dowson, Ernest, 141" — this single page is Sox's **entire** treatment of Dowson; a full-text search of the file confirms no other mention.

### L. Whose words are "first hyacinth since Douglas"? — Sox's endnote does not say

Endnote markers verified by zooming the rendered page: the marker after "live'." is **12**, the marker after "restore my reputation.'" is **13**. Sox's notes for Part V are printed at **p. 271** (repo PDF 303, folio confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03):

> 12. Also see Hart-Davis, *Selected Letters*, pp. 290-1.
>
> 13. Ellmann, *Oscar Wilde*, p. 507.

**Note 12 names no describer.** It is a cross-reference ("Also see"), and it points to Hart-Davis's 1962 *Selected Letters* pp. 290–1 — which, by Sox's own numbering (n. 7 = pp. 285–6, the 1 June 1897 letter to Ross; n. 8 = pp. 291–2), is Wilde's letter to Dowson of ? 16 June 1897, i.e. **passage B above**, the source of "the green costume" and "dark hyacinth locks" in Sox's *next* sentence. That letter does not contain the words "first hyacinth since Douglas".

So the phrase is left in an agentless passive ("Dowson **was described as**") with no attribution. It is demonstrably **not Wilde's own words** — it appears nowhere in the *Complete Letters* (full-text search of the OCR mirror: `hyacinth` occurs in this correspondence only as Wilde's "dark hyacinth locks") — and Sox does not present it as Fothergill's, since he names Fothergill explicitly for the adjacent comment in the very next sentence.

**The one other corpus source that uses the phrase cites Sox for it.** McKenna, *Secret Life* (2008 edn), writes "There was Dowson, Oscar's 'first hyacinth since Douglas'" (repo PDF p. 666) and his source note reads "'first hyacinth since Douglas': Sox, *Bachelors of Art*, page 141" (repo PDF p. 852; this file carries no printed folios — see `works.json`). The attribution therefore closes into a loop and terminates at Sox's unsourced passive.

**Conclusion, recorded as a null finding on attribution:** whose words these are is **not established** by any source read this session. They are not Wilde's; Sox does not say; McKenna points back at Sox. Anyone wanting the answer should check Ellmann's *Oscar Wilde* around pp. 505–7 (the pages Sox cites in nn. 6 and 13) and the Flower–Maas *Letters of Ernest Dowson* — neither is in the repo. **Because the phrase cannot be sourced to a participant, it cannot support a class above `second-hand`, and it is not used as class-deciding evidence in the edge.**

### M. The "cold mutton" / brothel story — provenance, and why it is not used

Sox's note 13 for this anecdote is "Ellmann, *Oscar Wilde*, p. 507". Frankel traces it further, and hedges it hard. `frankel-2017` **printed p. 114** (repo PDF 128, folio confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03):

> Within days of their first meeting, the two men were seeing each other regularly, and if rumor is to be believed, Dowson tried to interest Wilde in having sexual relations with women by taking him to a brothel: "It was like chewing cold mutton," Wilde is reputed to have said on leaving, "but tell it in England, where it will entirely restore my reputation."

Frankel's endnote 66, `frankel-2017` **printed p. 324** (repo PDF 338), gives the chain: the story is "Quoted in W. B. Yeats, *The Trembling of the Veil* (1922)"; Robert Sherard, in an unpublished 1937 letter to A. J. A. Symons (Clark Library), attributes Wilde's "agreeing to accompany Dowson to the brothel at Le Légué, Dieppe" to a wish to "conform to British bourgeois pretensions of morality"; but Sherard, writing to Hesketh Pearson in 1940 (Harry Ransom Center), calls "Yeats's yarn in *The Trembling of Veil* … a disgusting perversion of the truth", says "Dowson most certainly did not thus relate the incident", confirms the outing and the cold-mutton phrase, and adds "I could almost swear that it was O. W. who told me this when I was in Dieppe or Berneval in 1897."

Recorded, not promoted. It concerns Wilde's conduct with a third party, not the Wilde–Dowson connection, and its transmission is contested in the very note that preserves it. It is also the "sentence beyond what the owner quoted" that prompted this session, so it is transcribed here in full for the owner rather than summarised away.

### N. Frankel on the friendship — printed p. 113

`frankel-2017` **printed p. 113** (repo PDF 127; folio read in the text layer, page not separately rendered — cited `unverified` where used):

> Accompanying him to the Thaulows on June 23 was the dissolute poet Ernest Dowson, now one of his closest confidantes, whom Wilde had known back in London and who resided near Dieppe, at Arques-la-Bataille, until his departure for London in mid-July.

and, over the page break to printed 114:

> He remained ever-grateful for Dowson's frank and unguarded friendship in these difficult days … Dowson's premature death in 1900, just nine months before his own, was to affect him greatly.

Frankel elsewhere calls the friendship formative for the work: "Perhaps spurred by his sudden friendship with Dowson, as well as by his reading around this time of A. E. Housman's *A Shropshire Lad*, he made serious advances in the composition of *The Ballad of Reading Gaol*" (`frankel-2017` printed p. 121, repo PDF 135, text layer).

### O. Dowson's own voice — the other participant, via Frankel

The single most useful thing Frankel adds: he quotes **Dowson's own letters** on Wilde, from *Letters of Ernest Dowson*, ed. Desmond Flower and Henry Maas (Rutherford, NJ: Fairleigh Dickinson University Press, 1968) — his notes 64, 65 (p. 384), 68 (p. 387) and 90 (p. 390), at `frankel-2017` printed pp. 324–326. `frankel-2017` **printed p. 114** (repo PDF 128, folio confirmed on the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03):

> For his part, Dowson was impressed by Wilde's "enormous joy in life just at this moment" and "had some difficulty in suppressing my own sourness" as a result. His first impression was that Wilde had changed a great deal for the better: "he seems of much broader sympathies, much more human and simple. . . . His delight in the country, in walking, in the simplicities of life is enchanting."

and, same page:

> "His gorgeous spirits cheered me mightily," Dowson wrote of Wilde at this time: "I was amused by the unconscious contrast between his present talk about his changed position & his notions of economy & his practise, which is perversely extravagant."

**This is Dowson speaking about Wilde — participant testimony from the second endpoint of the pair.** Its register is admiring, affectionate, wry, and entirely non-romantic. Taken together with Wilde's letters, both participants are on record about the connection, and neither describes it as anything but friendship.

### P. McKenna's reading — the one scholarly source that names it love

`mckenna-2008` **repo PDF p. 665** (read as page image 2026-08-03; **no printed folio or running head appears on the rendered page**, consistent with the pagination warning already on this work in `works.json`):

> They met frequently, and their friendship suddenly seemed to deepen into love. It is unlikely that the relationship was ever sexual, but there may have been drunken embraces and declarations of undying love.

Explicitly speculative in its own wording, and `works.json` records that McKenna alone is never sufficient. Same page carries McKenna's flat statement that "Dowson's sexual interest lay in young girls" (PDF 666) and his fuller version of the brothel story, sourced to Dowson telling Yeats.

## The class proposal, and how it was reached

**Proposed: `attraction-expressed`, `direction: wilde`, `outcome: unreciprocated`, `certainty_status: proposed`.** Hidden by default on the map per the README; the person stays visible.

The README sets the bar: "Wilde's epistolary register is extravagant toward many correspondents; ardour on the page is not by itself an edge. An edge is earned when the desire is legible as desire to a reader who does **not** already hold the coding hypothesis, or when a scholarly source reads it that way." This pair clears it on both limbs.

**Legible without the hypothesis.** Inside a single letter (passage B): a declaration of inseparability, a compliment on the addressee's hair, absence named as the one imperfection in the day, and "Tout à toi". Across the fortnight: "You were wonderful and charming all last night"; "I want to be consoled"; twice an instruction on what to wear; "you have it, and other things, always".

**Read that way by a scholar.** McKenna, passage P.

**Why not `self-reported`** — i.e. a full relationship edge, as `marillier--wilde` has on a comparable correspondence. Because the counter-evidence is strong and comes from the better sources:

1. **Frankel**, the specialist monograph for exactly 1897–1900 and the most recent scholarship in this corpus, reads it as "frank and unguarded friendship" and Dowson as "one of his closest confidantes", and never gestures at romance — in a book that is otherwise thorough and unembarrassed about Wilde's exile-years liaisons.
2. **Dowson's own testimony** (passage O) is admiring, wry and non-romantic. Both participants are on record here, which is unusual for this map, and the second one does not corroborate.
3. Dowson's documented sexual interest was in women (McKenna PDF 666; the editors' "Mrs Dowson" footnote at *Letters* printed p. 1018 n. 3), and by both Sox's and Frankel's accounts he tried to steer Wilde toward women.
4. The ardent lines are a **fortnight's cluster** inside a correspondence that is otherwise about poems, absinthe, a translation and a debt. The Naples letters four months later are cold enough to be irritable.

**Why not a null finding (no edge), as for Turner.** The Turner record failed for want of (a) any source naming a romance and (b) any first-person ardour addressed to Turner himself — "every erotic subject in the whole correspondence is third-party". Neither is missing here: Wilde's ardour is addressed to Dowson directly, and McKenna names it. So Dowson clears the Turner bar, and the honest rendering is the hidden, arrow-marked flirtation edge rather than silence. That said, **dropping to no-edge would lose nothing that `dowson.json` and this dossier do not already carry**, and it is a defensible owner call.

**On `outcome`.** `unreciprocated` rather than `unknown` because Dowson's own side of the record survives and is not in kind, and because the one recorded rupture is Dowson cutting off the very curls Wilde had praised ("Wilde said they had quarrelled and Dowson, to spite him, 'had had all his long curls cut'", Sox p. 141). `unknown` is the defensible lower-commitment alternative, since no source records an approach being made and answered. `declined` is not available — there is nothing here like Fothergill's written withdrawal.

## Rejected candidates / null findings

**1. "First hyacinth since Douglas" — attribution not established.** The headline finding of this session. Sox prints it in an agentless passive; his endnote 12 names no describer and only cross-refers to Wilde's own 16 June letter, which does not contain the phrase; the phrase appears nowhere in the *Complete Letters* (full-text search of the OCR mirror: `hyacinth` occurs in this correspondence only in Wilde's own "dark hyacinth locks"); and McKenna, the only other corpus source to use it, cites Sox p. 141 for it. **Not Wilde's words; source unknown; not used as class-deciding evidence.** Next places to look, neither in the repo: Ellmann, *Oscar Wilde*, around pp. 505–7 (the pages Sox cites in his nn. 6 and 13), and *Letters of Ernest Dowson*, ed. Flower & Maas (1968). Full working at section L.

**2. No Dowson edge with anyone else on the roster.** Deliverable 4 returns nothing. Everyone Dowson is linked to in the sources read this session is **off-roster**: Leonard Smithers (publisher; the money conduit), Robert Sherard (the Oakley Street visit; the house he died in), Charles Conder, Dalhousie Young, Arthur Moore, Achille Fromentin, W. B. Yeats, the unnamed owner of the Café Royal, the unnamed "Mrs Dowson". Dowson appears in the *same rooms* as Douglas, Ross and Turner in 1897 but the sources record no connection between them beyond co-presence. One near-miss worth noting for its own sake and nothing more: Dowson is the source, in a letter to Arthur Moore of 2 February 1891, for the identification of John Gray as the original of Dorian (McKenna PDF 198, source note at PDF 755, citing Flower & Maas p. 182–183) — that is Dowson as a *witness* to the Gray–Wilde pair, not a party to anything. **No other person file or relationship file was created or touched.**

**3. Sox's chronology of the Dowson–Fothergill overlap is doubtful, and is not relied on.** Sox has Dowson among the guests at the Chalet Bourgeat during Fothergill's visit (pp. 140–141), and Fothergill's visit is placed by the `fothergill--wilde` record in Wilde's last month at Berneval (Wilde's "Rowland a sextette of suns", 21 Sept. 1897). But the *Letters* editors state at printed p. 924 n. 1 that "Dowson had returned to England in the middle of July." Sox's own hedge on the memoir applies — "highly disorganized", "few dates given" (p. 133). Sox's odd sentence "Although Dowson was in the house he did not appear" may indicate that Fothergill never actually met Dowson and is reporting Wilde's talk *about* him, which would fit both the quarrel and the chronology. Flagged, not resolved; the edge does not depend on the overlap being real.

**4. The brothel / "cold mutton" anecdote is recorded (section M) but not promoted.** It describes Wilde's conduct with a third party rather than the Wilde–Dowson connection, and Frankel's own endnote preserves Sherard calling Yeats's version "a disgusting perversion of the truth". Sox states it flatly ("Apparently, Dowson had also tried to persuade Wilde to try sexual relations with women again"); Frankel hedges it twice in one sentence ("if rumor is to be believed", "is reputed to have said"). Where it bears on this record at all, it bears *against* romance, and it is one strand of the basis for `outcome: unreciprocated`.

**5. Not pursued.** *Letters of Ernest Dowson*, ed. Flower & Maas (1968) — the obvious next primary source, and the one that would give Dowson's side directly rather than through Frankel's selection. Not in the repo, no IA id checked this session. Ellmann's *Oscar Wilde* likewise (relevant at pp. 505–7 and 547). Both are the recommended next steps if this record is taken past `phase-a`.
