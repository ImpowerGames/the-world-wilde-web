## mellor — Harold Mellor (1868–1925): host, patron, travelling companion; NOT a romantic edge

### M1 — Editors' potted biography (the bio-footnote, verified)

> 1. Harold Mellor (1868–1925) was the neurotic son of a Bolton cotton spinner. His father died in 1893, leaving him wealthy, and he spent most of the rest of his life abroad, finally committing suicide in his villa at Cannes. His aunt Ursula Mellor married Jacob Bright (1821–99), brother of John Bright and Liberal MP for Manchester 1867–95 with two short intervals.

— Holland & Hart-Davis, editors' footnote, _The Complete Letters of Oscar Wilde_ (2000), p. 1112n.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1158, printed folio 1112 confirmed at foot of the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Mellor's dates, wealth, expatriation, manner of death.
**Note for the record:** the footnote gives the death *year* only via the life-dates (1868–1925); it does not date the suicide separately, and it says "a Bolton cotton spinner" (not "cotton fortune" — the triage gist paraphrased). Place of death: his villa at Cannes.

### M2 — The meeting: Napoule, Christmas 1898 (Wilde to Ross)

> A nice fellow called Harold Mellor, who is staying at Cannes, comes over constantly to see me. He is a nephew of Mrs Jacob Bright's.¹ He has a pretty Italian boy with him. They stayed last night at Napoule, and we had plum-pudding and Mellor ordered Pommery-Greno, so I kept Christmas pleasantly, and Christmas improves by being kept a day. On the real Christmas I dined alone.
>
> The fishing population of the Riviera have the same freedom from morals as the Neapolitans have. They are very nice. Ever yours OSCAR

— Wilde to Robert Ross, Tuesday [27 December 1898], Hôtel des Bains, Napoule; _Complete Letters_, p. 1112.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1158, printed folio 1112 confirmed, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** first appearance of Mellor in the correspondence; the acquaintance begins at Napoule at Christmas 1898; the "pretty Italian boy" travelling with Mellor (= Eolo, per the editors' index). The fisher-population sentence is the same letter, immediately following.
**Register note:** "A nice fellow" is the warmest thing Wilde says about Mellor anywhere in the pages read this session — and it is the first mention. The register turns hostile from printed 1129 onward.

### M3 — Harrow, Eolo, and the fisherman (Wilde to Ross, 2 January 1899) — the single densest page

> No sign yet of Frank Harris: it is a great bore. There is a charming fellow called Harold Mellor (sent away from Harrow at the age of fourteen for being loved by the captain of the cricket eleven) who is staying with his mother near Cannes, and comes over on his bicycle to breakfast every morning. He is about twenty-six, but looks younger. Sometimes a very pretty, slim, fair-haired Italian boy bicycles over with him. His name is *Eolo*; his father, who sold him to Harold for 200 lire, having christened all his children – seventeen in number – out of the *Mythological Dictionary*. Harold is a nice fellow, but his boy bores him. It is dreadfully sad.
>
> He took me to Nice on Friday and we saw Sarah in *La Tosca*. … As regards my marrying again, I am quite sure that you will want me to marry this time some sensible, practical, plain, middle-aged boy, and I don't like the idea at all. Besides I am practically engaged to a fisherman of extraordinary beauty, age eighteen. So you see there are difficulties.

— Wilde to Robert Ross, 2 January 1899, Napoule; _Complete Letters_, p. 1116.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1162, printed folio 1116 confirmed at foot of the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision (one paragraph-internal elision marked; the elided run is the Sarah Bernhardt embrace and "I need you immensely", transcribed in full below)
**Supports:** (a) Mellor's own documented queerness, in Wilde's words — the Harrow expulsion; (b) what the letters record about Mellor's conduct toward the servant Eolo (purchase for 200 lire from the boy's father); (c) the Napoule fisherman item for the anonymous-liaison tier, with the age Wilde states.

Full text of the elided run, same letter and page:

> He took me to Nice on Friday and we saw Sarah in *La Tosca*. I went round to see Sarah and she embraced me and wept, and I wept, and the whole evening was wonderful. I wish to goodness you would come here. I need you immensely.

**Register note (load-bearing for classification):** "Harold is a nice fellow, but his boy bores him. It is dreadfully sad." — Wilde's sympathy in this sentence runs to Eolo's tedium at Mellor's hands, not to Mellor. Wilde's estimate "about twenty-six" is wrong against the editors' 1868 birth-year (Mellor would have been 30); recorded as Wilde's impression, not as a fact about Mellor.
**Sensitive-content register (README):** the letter states the fisherman's age as eighteen; Mellor was fourteen at the Harrow expulsion. Both stated exactly as the source states them, no gloss.

### M4 — "has Greek loves, and is rather ashamed of them" (Wilde to Turner, postmark 20 March 1899, Gland)

> The villa is pretty and comfortable: but I don't like Mellor: he is a silent, dull person, cautious, and economical: revolting Swiss wines appear at meals: he is complex without being interesting: has Greek loves, and is rather ashamed of them: has heaps of money, and lives in terror of poverty: so I regard it as a sort of Swiss *pension*, where there is no weekly bill.

— Wilde to Reginald Turner, [postmark 20 March 1899], Gland, Switzerland; _Complete Letters_, p. 1132.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1178, printed folio 1132 confirmed at foot of the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** (a) Mellor's own documented queerness, in Wilde's words, alongside his shame about it; (b) the hostile private register — the whole clause-chain is a catalogue of dislikes; (c) the patronage frame stated by Wilde himself: the villa as "a sort of Swiss *pension*, where there is no weekly bill."
**Classification note:** this sentence is the clearest single statement of what the Wilde–Mellor arrangement was, in Wilde's own accounting — free board, endured company. It is evidence *against* a romantic edge, not for one.

### M7 — The last visit: the "Mellor cure", July–August 1900, and Mellor's own surviving letter

> I have been with Harold Mellor at Gland: he is almost as neurasthenic as I am: but there was the automobile. I had to consult a specialist before I left Paris, I was so ill: it seems that not 'mussels' but neurasthenia were the cause of my illness, which had returned with renewed violence.¹

— Wilde to Robert Ross, [August 1900], Hôtel d'Alsace; _Complete Letters_, p. 1194.

Editors' footnote 1 on the same page, in full — **the only document by Mellor himself in this range:**

**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1240, printed folio 1194 confirmed at foot of the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact (the internal ellipsis in the footnote is the editors' own, printed as spaced periods).
**Supports:** the last Gland stay, dated by Mellor's letter to on-or-after Monday 30 July 1900; Mellor's solicitude in his own words; and the host's role as convalescent-keeper.
**Cross-thread note for the owner:** this page carries Wilde's own use of **neurasthenia** as a diagnosis for himself and for Mellor — directly relevant to the separate sexual-neurasthenia research thread, though nothing here connects the term to sexual aetiology.

### M8 — "his slave Eolo" and the late friendship (Wilde to Ross, 1 September 1900)

> The 'Mellor cure' was dull, but I got better. He is now in Paris with his slave Eolo, who like all slaves is most tyrannical. He and I, however, are great [ — sentence continues onto printed 1195; see M9]

— Wilde to Robert Ross, Saturday, 1 September [1900], [Paris]; _Complete Letters_, p. 1194.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1240, printed folio 1194 confirmed, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact for the transcribed words; the sentence is cut by the printed page break and is completed at M9.
**Supports:** what the letters record about Mellor's conduct toward Eolo, at the far end of the arc — six years after the "sold him to Harold for 200 lire" letter, Wilde is still describing Eolo as Mellor's property, now in the plain word "slave". Wilde's own framing is a joke ("who like all slaves is most tyrannical"), and the joke is recorded as Wilde's, not endorsed.

### M9 — The completion, and the 24-hour contradiction that settles the classification

Continuation of M8 across the printed page break:

> …friends. I think Harold is on the verge of acute melancholia. At present he has almost arrived at total abstinence – drinks and talks mineral waters. I like people who talk wine.

— Wilde to Robert Ross, Saturday, 1 September [1900], [Paris]; _Complete Letters_, pp. 1194–95. **So the full sentence reads: "He and I, however, are great friends."** — followed immediately by the melancholia diagnosis.

And, written the **next day** to a different correspondent:

> I am in my usual state, drifting along. I wish I had your concentration of purpose, and controlled intellect. My last visit to Mellor has given me melancholia. I must go no more. Ever yours OSCAR

— Wilde to Frank Harris, 2 September [1900], Hôtel d'Alsace; _Complete Letters_, p. 1195.
**Provenance (both):** repo PDF p. 1241, printed folio 1195 confirmed at foot of the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** the decisive register evidence. On 1 September Wilde tells Ross he and Mellor "are great friends"; on 2 September he tells Harris that the same visit "has given me melancholia. I must go no more." The late warmth is real but audience-dependent, and it is warmth about a *host*.
**This resolves the brief's "we are great friends" pointer:** the phrase exists, at printed 1194–95, in the form "He and I, however, are great friends" — and it is bracketed on one side by "his slave Eolo" and on the other by "on the verge of acute melancholia", with the Harris recantation 24 hours later. It is not evidence of romance and should not be quoted without its neighbours.

## didaco — Didaco: the Genoa liaison, February–March 1899

### D1 — The whole item, verbatim (Wilde to Turner, postmark 20 March 1899)

> On my way I stopped at Genoa, where I met a beautiful young actor, a Florentine, whom I wildly loved. He has the strange name of Didaco. He had the look of Romeo, without Romeo's sadness: a face chiselled for high romance. We spent three days together.

— Wilde to Reginald Turner, [postmark 20 March 1899], Gland, Switzerland; _Complete Letters_, p. 1132.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1178, printed folio 1132 confirmed at foot of the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact. This is the closing paragraph on the printed page; see D2 for the continuation check.
**Supports:** a named, dated, first-person-attested liaison of the exile years — Wilde's own account of loving Didaco and of three days spent together.

**CORRECTION to the brief and to the triage:** the place is **Genoa**, not Naples. "On my way I stopped at Genoa" — i.e. en route to Gland, on the February 1899 journey from the Riviera into Switzerland; the letter recounting it is postmarked 20 March 1899 from Gland. Any Naples attribution for Didaco is an error.

**Age:** the letter states **no age**. It says "a beautiful young actor, a Florentine" — "young" and nothing more. Nothing in the passage supports a numeric age, and none should be inferred.

**Classification weighing (proposed, not decided).** Against the README taxonomy:
- `documented-sexual` requires *sexual relations attested* by first-person primary evidence. This is first-person primary evidence by a participant, but what it attests in plain words is love and three days of company. A reader holding the coding hypothesis will read "We spent three days together", in a letter to Turner in the series that elsewhere calls Riviera romance "a profession plied beneath the moon", as reporting a sexual liaison. That is a reading, and naming whose reading it is is required — it is mine as transcriber, not the editors'. Not proposed as the class.
- `consensus` requires ≥2 independent secondary sources; none consulted. Not available.
- `attraction-expressed` is defined as "an approach or declaration, **not a relationship**". Three days together is more than an approach, and the outcome is not unknown — they spent the time together. Poor fit.
- **`romantic-nonsexual` is the proposed class**: documented romance where the record makes no sexual claim either way — which is exactly the evidentiary situation. Per the README the "nonsexual" in the id describes the evidence, not a finding of fact.
- **One-sidedness caveat:** the recorded affect is Wilde's ("whom I wildly loved"); Didaco's side of it is not recorded anywhere in the passage. The mutual element is conduct, not sentiment: "We spent three days together."

### D2 — Continuation check (printed 1133)

The Turner letter runs over onto printed 1133 and ends there — "Write and tell me some of your adventures, and what new stars have crossed your way. Are you in love? If not, why not? … I wait on Fortune, like a discarded lover. Ever yours OSCAR" — with **no further mention of Didaco**. D1 is therefore the complete record of the liaison on this page-pair.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 1179, printed folio 1133 confirmed at foot of the rendered page, read as page image 2026-08-03.

## rolla — Edoardo Rolla: Genoa, April 1899

### R1 — The whole item, verbatim (Wilde to Ross, from Gland, early April 1899)

> I hope to find at Genoa, waiting for me, a young lad, by name Edoardo Rolla, one of the sea-farers. He has fair hair, and is always in dark blue. I have written to him. After the chill virginity of Swiss Alps and snow, I long for the red flowers of life that stain the feet of summer in Italy. Ever yours O. W.

— Wilde to Robert Ross, [early April 1899], Gland; _Complete Letters_, p. 1140. (Final paragraph of the letter; the two Ross postcards immediately following on the same printed page are postmarked 7 April 1899 from Santa Margherita, dating the Genoa move to the first week of April.)

**Supports:** an anticipated meeting arranged by letter with a named young man at Genoa; Wilde's stated longing; no recorded outcome.

**Departure context, same letter, same page (Mellor):**

> … Furies of insanity that beset his race. I really was very sorry for him, but I could not stay. However, we parted amicably on my side, and on his with protestations of admiration and remorse.
>
> I leave tomorrow morning for Genoa – Albergo di Firenze – a small inn on the quay, rather *mal-famé* but cheap.

**Provenance:** same page (opening lines run on from printed 1139, not read this session — the transcription begins mid-sentence and is marked with a leading elision).
**Supports:** the end of the first Gland stay; Mellor's distress at Wilde's departure; the Genoa destination that frames the Rolla item.

**Classification weighing (proposed, not decided).** `attraction-expressed`, `direction: "wilde"`, `outcome: "unknown"`.
- **Against the README bar** — "legible as desire to a reader who does **not** already hold the coding hypothesis": in my judgment yes, and the reason is the sentence Wilde puts immediately after the boy: "After the chill virginity of Swiss Alps and snow, I long for the red flowers of life…". The physical description ("fair hair… always in dark blue"), the fact that Wilde has **written ahead** to arrange the meeting, and an explicit statement of longing set against "virginity" sit in one four-sentence run. A reader without the hypothesis reads an older man looking forward to a young man he has written to. What such a reader would *not* independently supply is the sexual character — that part is coded, and I am not proposing it.
- **Whose reading:** mine as transcriber. No scholarly source consulted reads the passage for the record. The editors' general index lists him, per the triage's mirror read, as "Rolla, Edoardo (boy)" — an editorial classing, not a reading of desire, and finding-only until index-verified at page.
- **Outcome is genuinely `unknown`**, not `unreciprocated`: nothing in the pages read says whether the meeting happened. The only thing known to have happened at Genoa in this window is the Didaco liaison (D1), which the 20 March letter places on the *earlier* February journey — a different stop, and Didaco is a Florentine actor, not "one of the sea-farers". They are two people; do not merge them.
