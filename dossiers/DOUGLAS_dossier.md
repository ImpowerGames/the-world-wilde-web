## wilde — the central relationship, 1891/92–1900

**Passage 0a — the editors' headnote on the meeting (Douglas's main particulars; italic interchapter note, printed p. 461)**

> It was in the year 1891 that there entered Wilde's life the man who was to be in some sense his inspiration and in every sense his evil genius.   Lord Alfred Bruce Douglas, the third son of the eighth Marquess of Queensberry, had been born in 1870, educated at Winchester and was now a second-year undergraduate at Magdalen College, Oxford.   Lionel Johnson, a Winchester contemporary and Oxford friend, brought him to see Wilde at Tite Street.   Douglas says this momentous meeting took place in 1891, in the vacation, which seems likely.

— Holland & Hart-Davis, editorial note, *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 461.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 481 (printed p. 461, folio checked), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Douglas born 1870; third son of the 8th Marquess of Queensberry; Winchester + Magdalen; introduced to Wilde by Lionel Johnson, 1891.

**Passage 0b — Wilde's own dating (quoted by the editors in the same note, from his letter to More Adey of 7 April 1897)**

> The friendship began in May 1892 by his brother appealing to me in a very pathetic letter to help him in terrible trouble with people who were blackmailing him.   I hardly knew him at the time.   I had known him eighteen months, but had only seen him four times in that space.

— Wilde to More Adey, 7 April 1897, as quoted in the editorial note, *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 461 (the letter itself at p. 795, not read this session).
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 481 (printed p. 461, folio checked), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Wilde dated the *friendship* proper from May 1892 (the blackmail appeal); acquaintance ran ~18 months before that with only four meetings.
**Editors' conclusion in the same note (≤2 sentences):** "This does not rule out the possibility of their first meeting at the end of the Oxford Trinity term around the beginning of July [1891], which seems the most likely date." Also records the Clark Library *Dorian Gray* large-paper copy "inscribed 'Alfred Douglas from his friend who wrote this book.   July 91.   Oscar'", and Douglas's 1925 letter to Harris: "From the second time he saw me … he made 'overtures' to me."

— Holland & Hart-Davis, editorial note (n. 1 on Lionel Johnson), *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 423 n.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 445 (printed p. 423, folio checked), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact

**Passage 0d — "I worship him", May–June 1892**

> My dearest Bobbie, Bosie has insisted on stopping here for sandwiches.   He is quite like a narcissus – so white and gold.   I will come either Wednesday or Thursday night to your rooms.   Send me a line.   Bosie is so tired: he lies like a hyacinth on the sofa, and I worship him.
>
> You dear boy.   Ever yours
>
> OSCAR

— Wilde to Robert Ross, [? May–June 1892], Royal Palace Hotel, Kensington. *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 526.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 546 (printed p. 526, folio checked), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Earliest surviving intimate register about Douglas ("I worship him"), told to Ross, spring 1892 — consistent with Wilde's own May-1892 dating of the friendship's start.
**Editorial notes at same page (≤2 sentences each):** n. 1: "This letter cannot be dated exactly, but it seems likely to have been written fairly soon after May 1892 (see p.461)." · n. 2: "The nickname (a contraction of Boysie) by which Lord Alfred Douglas was known to his family and friends from early childhood."

— Wilde to Lord Alfred Douglas, [? November 1892], no address printed. *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 538.

**Passage 2 — the "Hyacinthus" letter (read out at the trials)**

> My Own Boy, Your sonnet is quite lovely, and it is a marvel that those red rose-leaf lips of yours should have been made no less for music of song than for madness of kisses. >
> Why are you alone in London, and when do you go to Salisbury?   Do go there to cool your hands in the grey twilight of Gothic things, and come here whenever you like.   It is a lovely place – it only lacks you; but go to Salisbury first.   Always, with undying love, yours
>
> OSCAR

— Wilde to Lord Alfred Douglas, [? January 1893], [Babbacombe Cliff]. *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 544.

**Passage 6 — Wilde to Lady Queensberry on Bosie, 8 November 1893**

> Dear Lady Queensberry, You have on more than one occasion consulted me about Bosie.   Let me write to you now about him.
>
> Bosie seems to me to be in a very bad state of health.   He is sleepless, nervous, and rather hysterical.   He seems to me quite altered. …  He does absolutely nothing, and is quite astray in life, and may, unless you or Drumlanrig do something, come to grief of some kind. …  Why not try and make arrangements of some kind for him to go abroad for four or five months, to the Cromers in Egypt if that could be managed … 
>
> I like to think myself his greatest friend – he, at any rate, makes me think so – so I write to you quite frankly to ask you to send him abroad to better surroundings.   It would save him, I feel sure.   At present his life seems to me to be tragic and pathetic in its foolish aimlessness.
>
> You will not, I know, let him know *anything about my letter*.   I can rely on you, I feel sure.   Sincerely yours

— Wilde to Lady Queensberry, [8 November 1893], 16 Tite Street. *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 575.

### Arrest and trials cluster, April–May 1895 (printed 645–652 = PDF 696–703; offset +51 here — one PDF page lost vs the +52 region)

**Passage 8 — Holloway, 29 April 1895, eve of the first Crown trial**

> My dearest boy, This is to assure you of my immortal, my eternal love for you.   Tomorrow all will be over.   If prison and dishonour be my destiny, think that my love for you and this idea, this still more divine belief, that you love me in return will sustain me in my unhappiness and will make me capable, I hope, of bearing my grief most patiently. …  As for you (graceful boy with a Christ-like heart), as for you, I beg you, as soon as you have done all that you can, leave for Italy and regain your calm, and write those lovely poems which you do with such a strange grace.   Do not expose yourself to England for any reason whatsoever.   If one day, at Corfu or in some enchanted isle, there were a little house where we could live together, oh! life would be sweeter than it has ever been.

— Wilde to Lord Alfred Douglas, Monday Evening [29 April 1895], HM Prison, Holloway. *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 646.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 697 (printed p. 646, folio checked), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision
**Supports:** "that you love me in return" — Wilde's assertion of mutual love at the moment of catastrophe; the shared-house fantasy.
**Editorial provenance caveat (p. 646 n. 1, ≤2 sentences):** "The text of this letter, and of the ones on pp.650 and 651, is based on Mason's version, but words and phrases have been altered, following the French, into what seems more like Wilde's language, and the versions of several sentences given by Douglas in his *Autobiography* (1929) have been followed rather than Mason.   All this may well have slightly distorted Wilde's words, but their substance need not be doubted." — Same note: "Douglas says that he later destroyed 150 of Wilde's letters to him, including those from Holloway."

### De Profundis — the prison letter to Douglas, Jan–Mar 1897 (printed 683–686 = PDF 734–737, folios checked)

**Passage 13 — opening address**

> Dear Bosie, After long and fruitless waiting I have determined to write to you myself, as much for your sake as for mine, as I would not like to think that I had passed through two long years of imprisonment without ever [p. 684:] having received a single line from you, or any news or message even, except such as gave me pain.

— Wilde to Lord Alfred Douglas, [January–March 1897], HM Prison, Reading. *Complete Letters* (2000), pp. 683–4.
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 734–5 (printed pp. 683–4, folios checked), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact (page-break bridged, no elision)
**Supports:** The prison letter is addressed to Douglas personally; total silence between them during the sentence is Wilde's own premise (see caution note below).

**Passage 14 — the friendship named and mourned**

> Our ill-fated and most lamentable friendship has ended in ruin and public infamy for me, yet the memory of our ancient affection is often with me, and the thought that loathing, bitterness and contempt should for ever take that place in my heart once held by love is very sad to me …

— Wilde to Lord Alfred Douglas, [January–March 1897], HM Prison, Reading. *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 684.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 735 (printed p. 684, folio checked), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision
**Supports:** Wilde's own retrospective naming of the bond — "that place in my heart once held by love".

**Passage 15 — the 1893 daily routine (cohabitation-in-practice)**

> I arrived at St James's Place every morning at 11.30, in order to have the opportunity of thinking and writing without the interruptions inseparable from my own household, quiet and peaceful as that household was.   But the attempt was vain.   At twelve o'clock you drove up, and stayed smoking cigarettes and chattering till 1.30, when I had to take you out to luncheon at the Café Royal or the Berkeley.   Luncheon with its *liqueurs* lasted usually till 3.30.   For an hour you retired to White's.   At tea-time you appeared again, and stayed till it was time to dress for dinner.   You dined with me either at the Savoy or at Tite Street.   We did not separate as a rule till after midnight, as supper at Willis's had to wind up the entrancing day.   That was my life for those three months, every single day, except during the four days when you went abroad.   I then, of course, had to go over to Calais to fetch you back.

— Wilde to Lord Alfred Douglas (the prison letter), recalling September–November 1893. *Complete Letters* (2000), p. 686.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 737 (printed p. 686, folio checked), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Day-in-day-out inseparability in 1893 as Wilde himself narrates it; also "with but few intervals you were, I regret to say, by my side always" (p. 685).

**Editorial apparatus recorded (each ≤2 sentences):**
- p. 683 n. 1: "In addition, Ross removed in all more than a thousand words, almost all of them fiercely critical of Douglas and his father: the description of Lord Queensberry in court (see p.758) is a striking example.   Wilde's letter is printed here exactly as he wrote it, except that it has been divided up into rather more paragraphs than his scanty ration of paper allowed him." — same note: the letter was never posted from Reading; Wilde handed it to Ross the day after release (see p. 780 n., p. 842).
- p. 684 n. 1 (CAUTION — bears on how De Profundis may be used as evidence): "Wilde's indictments of Douglas in this letter need to be approached with caution.   While some are undoubtedly valid, others, in the light of later research, seem both unfair to Douglas and inaccurate if taken entirely at face value." — same note cites a Douglas-to-Ada-Leverson letter of 13 September 1895 showing Douglas "distraught at being unable to communicate with Wilde".

**Rejected candidates (kept for audit trail):** p. 685 "I blame myself for allowing an unintellectual friendship … to entirely dominate my life" and p. 686 "When I compare my friendship with you to my friendship with such still younger men as John Gray and Pierre Louÿs I feel ashamed" — recrimination passages; establish tone of the letter but the relationship-attestation work is done by Passages 13–15; the Gray comparison belongs to the gray--wilde researcher if wanted.

### Post-prison and Naples reunion, June–September 1897 (printed 871–887 = PDF 921–937; printed 929–936 = PDF 979–986; offset +50 in both — folios checked)

**Passage 16 — Berneval, 4 June 1897: love affirmed, meeting refused**

> My dear Boy, I have just got your letter … I love the last words of anything: the end in art is the beginning.   Don't think I don't love you.   Of course I love you more than anyone else.   But our lives are irreparably severed, as far as meeting goes.   What is left to us is the knowledge that we love each other, and every day I think of you, and I know you are a poet, and that makes you doubly dear and wonderful. … [p. 881:] Ever, dear boy, with fondest love.
>
> OSCAR

— Wilde to Lord Alfred Douglas, Friday, 4 June [1897], 2.30, [Hôtel de la Plage, Berneval-sur-Mer]. *Complete Letters* (2000), pp. 880–81.
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 930–31 (printed pp. 880–81, folios checked), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision
**Supports:** Two weeks after release Wilde declares continuing mutual love while holding that they cannot meet — the tension the Naples reunion later resolves.

**Passage 17 — Berneval, 6 June 1897: writing to Douglas daily**

> My dearest Boy, I must give up this *absurd* habit of writing to you every day.   It comes of course from the strange new joy of talking to you daily. … [p. 886:] Dear boy, I hope you are still sweetly asleep: you are so absurdly sweet when you are asleep. …  Remember, after a few days, only *one letter a week*.   I *must* school myself to it.
>
> *En attendant*, yours with all love
>
> OSCAR

**Passage 18 — Dieppe, [? 31 August 1897]: the decision to return to Douglas**

> My own Darling Boy, I got your telegram half an hour ago, and just send you a line to say that I feel that my only hope of again doing beautiful work [p. 933:] in art is being with you.   It was not so in old days, but now it is different, and you can really recreate in me that energy and sense of joyous power on which art depends.   Everyone is furious with me for going back to you, but they don't understand us.   I feel that it is only with you that I can do anything at all.   Do remake my ruined life for me, and then our friendship and love will have a different meaning to the world.
>
> I wish that when we met at Rouen we had not parted at all.   There are such wide abysses now of space and land between us.   But we love each other.   Goodnight, dear.   Ever yours
>
> OSCAR

— Wilde to Lord Alfred Douglas, Tuesday, 7.30 [? 31 August 1897], Café Suisse, Dieppe. *Complete Letters* (2000), pp. 932–3.
**Provenance:** repo PDF pp. 982–3 (printed pp. 932–3, folios checked), read as page images 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact (page-break bridged)
**Supports:** The reunion decision in Wilde's own words, against his circle's opposition ("Everyone is furious with me for going back to you").
**Editorial note (p. 933 n. 1, ≤2 sentences + quoted primary):** "This fateful meeting seems to have taken place on 28–9 August.   In his *Autobiography* Douglas wrote: 'Poor Oscar cried when I met him at the station.   We walked about all day arm in arm, or hand in hand, and were perfectly happy.'"
