## Printed 100 — Adelaide Road: the household's turnover, Freddie Smith, and the Goddard daughters

### A. Freddie Smith → Robbie Ross (the Ives-circle ↔ Wilde-circle bridge)

**Passage 1**

> Also in the house were Pug and Elephant and other intimate male friends of Ives who became housemates for often extended periods before moving on – often to marry, or in the case of Freddie Smith to move in with Robbie Ross who was a good friend of both Ives and Wilde.

— Matt Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (2014), p. 100.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 111 (printed folio 100 confirmed on the rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** (a) Freddie Smith was a resident member of the Adelaide Road household; (b) he left it to live with Robert Ross; (c) Cook places Ross as a close friend of both Ives and Wilde. This is the single most direct household-level link between the Ives circle and the Wilde circle found in ch. 4.
**Note on the sentence's grammar:** Freddie Smith is named as a case of the "other intimate male friends of Ives who became housemates" — i.e. Cook's own framing puts him in the same class as Pug and Elephant, not in the servant class (Kit/Sylvie). "Intimate male friends" is Cook's phrase and is *not* glossed by him as sexual. **Classification proposed, not decided.**

**Immediately preceding context (same paragraph, for the framing of the departures):**

> Kit and his wife Sylvie kept house for Ives in a relatively familiar – though rapidly diminishing – tradition of live-in domestic service.

— Cook (2014), p. 100. Same provenance. **Supports:** the Goddards' position in the household is domestic service, distinct from the "intimate male friends" cohort.

**Immediately following context (the household's self-understanding):**

> These housemates appear to have moved with relative ease between these positions, something Ives seems to have both expected and, with some sadness, accepted. Ives saw them all as his 'family' – a family connected by co-residence rather than by blood ties.

— Cook (2014), p. 100. Same provenance. Endnote 21 attaches here.

### B. The Goddard daughters' departure, and Ives choosing "Elephant"

**Passage 2**

> James and Sylvie Goddard remained with Ives until their deaths; their daughters until the early 1940s. They could no longer put up with the Elephant's alcoholism, and left to open a shop using money Ives had settled on their father. Ives effectively chose Elephant over them – even though, as a teetotaller, his drinking was a source of considerable angst for him too.

— Matt Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (2014), p. 100.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 111 (printed folio 100 confirmed on the rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** dates the daughters' departure to the early 1940s; gives the cause (Saunders's alcoholism); and gives Cook's own characterisation of Ives's choice ("effectively chose Elephant over them"). Endnote 22 attaches at "him too."
**Status of "chose Elephant over them":** this is Cook's interpretive gloss, in his own voice, on an outcome — it is **not** a statement that Ives declared a preference. Record as Cook's characterisation.

**Passage 3 — Therese Goddard, 1944**

> The sisters nevertheless continued to visit Adelaide Road regularly and when one of them, Therese, went in 1944 she was described by Ives as 'the youngest of my adopted family – and she is 47!'.

— Matt Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (2014), p. 100.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 111 (printed folio 100 confirmed on the rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** the daughters' relationship with Ives survived the departure; gives Therese Goddard's implied birth year (c. 1897) and Ives's own "adopted family" language. Endnote 23 attaches.
**Reading note:** "when one of them, Therese, went in 1944" is ambiguous on the page between "visited" and "died"; the surrounding sentence favours "visited". ⧖ Flagged, not resolved.

### H. Printed 97–98 — birth family (summary, no household members)

Printed 97–98 concern Ives's natal family, not the household: his early care by a nurse in Croydon before moving at about five to his paternal grandmother the Hon. Emma Ives; his calling her "mother" throughout the diary; meeting his birth mother only twice, the second time in 1933 after years of trying to trace her; the accepted distance from his father; and his father's second wife Millicent, who thought him "mad" at first and "a dangerous influence" before he "won her over to be an affectionate friend". Cook also gives the diary's scope here (1886–1950, 122 volumes, c. 3 million words) — already in `ives.json` from Cook 2003.

**No named partners or housemates on these pages.** Verified at repo PDF pp. 108–109 (printed folios 97 and 98 confirmed on the rendered pages), read as page images 2026-08-03.

Two named-person details sit just *before* this cluster, on printed 96 (already mined, recorded here only so the roster owner sees the names): the half-brothers **Victor** and **Cecil**, from Colonel Gordon-Ives's 1880 marriage to **Amy Pullen**, born when George was 13. Bentworth Hall (Hampshire) and the Villa Ives (Nice) are named there as the family homes.

**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 112 (printed folio 101; this folio was confirmed on the rendered page by the earlier pass that mined the same page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision (Cook's own ellipsis, reproduced)

**Passage 10**

> The Goddards (Kit and Sylvie) were the conjugal heterosexual couple in the Adelaide Road household who might theoretically have displaced Ives in these maternal/paternal roles. Ives' class and money prevented this, however, and these factors stood before sex and coupledom in the household hierarchy.

— Matt Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (2014), p. 102.
**Provenance:** as Passage 11 · verification: verified-exact

> The Adelaide Road set-up was not created out of immediately comprehensible family ties and as a result the various relationships could have been understood in a number of ways. ### K. Printed 103 — the cleaner's testimony (the household after the women left)

**Passage 14**

> With Ives, it was only after Sylvie died and her daughters had left that we can find comments about the eccentricity – and the queerness – of the Ives household. With no live-in women 'to do', Ives employed a cleaner. Ives, she said, 'was very nice, very quiet, always sticking things into his black and gold books [his scrapbooks]. He was "queer", and lived with two men […] I think he kept them both'.

— Matt Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (2014), p. 103.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 114 (printed folio 103 confirmed on the rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-elision (Cook's own ellipsis and bracketed gloss, reproduced)
**Supports:** a **contemporary outsider's** reading of the household as queer, and of the two remaining men as financially kept. Endnote 47 attaches. **This is the closest thing in ch. 4 to third-party testimony about the nature of the household**, as against Cook's own inference from the diaries.
**Who the "two men" are:** unstated on this page. The chronology (post-Sylvie's death, post-daughters' departure, i.e. after the early 1940s) and the will on printed 118 — which names Harold Bloodworth and Stanley Saunders as the two men Ives was living with — together **suggest** Pug and Elephant. ⧖ **Inference, not stated by Cook. Flagged, not resolved.**

**Cook's own gloss on the same page**

> Here was a homosexual and two 'kept' men languishing in a house of rotting fruit. The respectable household of the interwar years had turned decidedly queer postwar.

— Cook (2014), p. 103. Same provenance · verification: verified-exact. Cook is voicing the outsider's perception, not endorsing it.

Also on printed 103: Cook contrasts Ives with **Edward Carpenter**, named as "Ives' friend", who set up home with **George Merrill** at Millthorpe in 1898 — Cook quotes Carpenter on radical friends "appalled at the absence of a 'women's touch'". **Passage 15 — Ives's own attitude to the Wilde/Symonds/Ashbee arrangements**

> He knew and/or was strongly influenced by a number of men who saw themselves as inverts or homosexuals and yet who also married, had children, and took that parental role seriously – John Addington Symonds, Oscar Wilde and Charles Ashbee amongst them. These men wove these parts of their lives together with varying degrees of candour, and Ives – often quick to judge – made no adverse comment about their arrangements.

— Matt Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (2014), p. 104.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 115 (printed folio 104 confirmed on the rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact

## Printed 107 — "Brothers-in-arms": the Order of Chaeronea as a form of family

### O. Cook's thesis sentence linking bed-sharing and the Order

**Passage 20**

> More horizontal notions of brotherhood and comradeship were important too – in the way he described sharing a bed with his male housemates, for example, and especially in the way he conceptualised the Order of the Chaeronea and his relationships with his fellow middle and upper-class champions of the 'cause'.

— Matt Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (2014), p. 107.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 118 (printed folio 107 confirmed on the rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Cook explicitly ties the household bed-sharing and the Order together as two expressions of one idiom — horizontal comradeship as against vertical paternity. **This is the interpretive hinge for reading the Ives-household edges: it argues the bed-sharing is being read by Cook as fraternal-comradely, not as evidence of sexual pairing.** Directly relevant to the certainty language in `holt--ives.json`, `bloodworth--ives.json`, `goddard--ives.json`, `ives--saunders.json`.

### P. The Order's non-hierarchical structure

**Passage 21**

> Though Ives was at the centre of the Order in terms of its foundation around 1893, he also specifically structured it non-hierarchically. Any two existing members could invite a third, theoretically creating a level playing field of brothers rather than establishing a hierarchy with presidents, vice presidents, committees and subcommittees.

— Cook (2014), p. 107. Same provenance · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** dates the founding to "around 1893" (consistent with the Complete Letters footnote "in about 1893" already in `ives.json`), and gives the recruitment mechanism — **two existing members could invite a third.** That mechanism is directly useful to the roster: Order membership implies a sponsoring pair, so any attested member had two existing members vouching for him. ⧖ **Cook does not name any sponsor pairs here.**

### Q. The Order as a "family"

**Passage 22**

> The ritual for joining the Order meanwhile emphasised mutual responsibility, duty, loyalty and endurance. Such rhetoric was familiar from other models of political organising, but by ritualising the order along almost Masonic lines, Ives inculcated a sense of unconditionally [sic] and permanence which brushed closely against notions of family.

— Cook (2014), p. 107. Same provenance · verification: verified-exact (the malformed "unconditionally" is Cook's, transcribed as printed)
**Transcription note:** a first pass off the full-page image dropped "meanwhile" and the hedge "almost" before "Masonic". Both were restored after a 6× zoom crop of the paragraph (repo PDF p. 118, lower half), read 2026-08-03. **The hedge matters — Cook says "almost Masonic", not "Masonic".**
**Supports:** the Order as quasi-kinship, on the Masonic model. Endnote 70 attaches at "endurance."

**Passage 23 — the key formulation for the "Order as family" target**

> It helped to suggest less a voluntary allegiance and more a pre-existing bond of sexuality, a homosexual 'family', which Ives saw the ritual acknowledging rather than creating.

— Cook (2014), p. 107. Same provenance · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** Cook's clearest statement that Ives conceived the Order as a family constituted by shared sexuality and merely *recognised* by the ritual — not created by it. Follows with the Theban Band lineage: "an elite family with a heroic lineage, stretching back to the Theban Bands, whose battle at Chaeronea in 338 BCE Ives commemorated in the name he selected (a reference point for Derek Jarman too)" (endnote 71), and closes "It was a family with historical depth and one which commanded his allegiance as surely as his relatives and household did."

Also on printed 107: the **British Society for the Study of Sex Psychology** is given with offices — "Ives was treasurer and Carpenter life president" — and Cook traces the organisational line forward to the postwar **Homosexual Law Reform Society** and **Campaign for Homosexual Equality**. Note again Cook's spelling **"the Order of the Chaeronea"**.

## Printed 109–110 — the will of October 1939: the household priced and ranked

Cook reads Ives's will (October 1939, eight typed pages, ten codicils over the following decade) as a document of chosen kinship. This is the single most concrete evidence in ch. 4 for how Ives himself ranked the household.

### T. The Goddard daughters as "adopted daughters"

**Passage 27**

> Therese and Adele Goddard – his 'adopted daughters' – each receive £400; their mother, Sylvie, £60. This bequest was reapportioned after Sylvie's death, and the gifts to the girls were revoked, rebalanced (in favour of Therese – perhaps his favourite of the two, perhaps the most in need), and eventually largely reinstated in codicils.

— Matt Cook, _Queer Domesticities_ (2014), p. 109.
**Provenance:** repo PDF p. 120 (printed folio 109 confirmed on the rendered page), read as page image 2026-08-03 · verification: verified-exact
**Supports:** confirms both daughters' names and Ives's own "adopted daughters" formula; shows the bequests to them were revoked and then largely reinstated — which reads against the printed-100 account of a clean break. The revocation plausibly corresponds to the early-1940s departure. ⧖ **Cook does not connect the two events; the correlation is mine and is flagged, not asserted.**

### U. Bloodworth and Saunders inherit the house; Tilly Hayes next in line

**Passage 28**

> The two men he was living with – Harold Bloodworth and Stanley Saunders (Pug and Elephant) – were left the Adelaide Road house and contents; Tilly Hayes was next in line to inherit after them.
